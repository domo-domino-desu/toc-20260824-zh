\begin{center}
  \large\textbf{A note about these answers}
\end{center}

My friend William Zwicker from Union College tells a story about that
he calls the
Clock Problem Problem.
An example of a Clock Problem is:~it is 4:38~pm on Tuesday.
Between now and 5:15~am Saturday, how many times will a clock's minute hand pass
its hour hand?

Bill saw a writeup of a complete solution to this class of problems.
However, he said, the writeup's problem is that 
we don't ask this question for the answer.
The point of this problem is that we can give it to any bright student,
in complete precision.
That is, the point of some problems 
is simply for students to do them.
 
This captures the dilemma facing a person who provides answers to 
text exercises.
Making them complete answers has risks, including that
some students will just read the question and then read the answer, and
so proceed with the incorrect sense that they understand how to do it.

Weighing against that is the worry that incomplete answers, or hints,
or incomplete lists (such as odd only), can leave a honestly-trying person 
without a way to see what it was that they didn't see.

For this book I have provided complete answers.
Many of the readers are people doing the work on their own and my judgement is 
that they are helped more by the opportunity, in the absence of an instructor,
to see things that they otherwise wouldn't see.
