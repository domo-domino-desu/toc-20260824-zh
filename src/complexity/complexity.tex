We will review the basics of the orders of growth of functions.
Because this is a review, we will not give a detailed development, just enough for our needs.
And we will leave the proofs for the exercises.

\section{Definition}
For a problem there may be many algorithms.
We typically want to choose one that is faster or uses less memory,
or less of some other resource.
The rate at which algorithms use resources 
is a function of the size of their input.
We have tools to describe the rates.

First, an anecdote.
Here is the grade school multiplication algorithm.
% How long does it take?
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{array}{@{}r@{}}
    678\\
    \times 42\\  \hline
    1356\\
   +27120\\  \hline
   28476
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
For each digit of the multiplier~$42$ we 
multiply each digit of the multiplicand~$678$.
That's a nested loop.
The natural guess is that multiplication of two $n$-bit numbers
requires, at worst, a time of about $n^2$ ticks.

% https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Andrej_Nikolajewitsch_Kolmogorov.jpg
\margingraphic{0.20\textwidth}{complexity/Kolmogorov.jpg}{\label{fig:kolmogorov}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov}{Andrey Kolmogorov 1903--1987}}
In 1960 A~Kolmogorov organized a seminar at Moscow State University with the
goal of proving this.
Within a week one of his students,
\citetext{A~Karatsuba}{See \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Karatsuba}.},
had found that it is false.
Karatsuba produced a 
\citetext{clever algorithm}{%
The idea is to let $k = \ceiling{n/2}$ 
and write $x = x_1 2^k + x_0$ and $y = y_1 2^k + y_0$
(so $678=21 \cdot 2^5+6$ and $42=1\cdot 2^5+10$). 
Then we have $xy = A\cdot 2^{2k} + B\cdot 2^k + C$ 
where $A = x_1 y_1$, and $B = x_1 y_0 + x_0 y_1$, and~$C = x_0 y_0$
(here, $28\,476=21\cdot 2^{10}+216\cdot 2^5+60$). 
The multiplications by $2^{2k}$ and $2^{k}$ are just bit-shifts to known 
locations independent of the values of $x$ and $y$, 
so they don't affect the time much. 
But the two multiplications for 
$B$ seem remove all the advantage and still give $\Theta(n^2)$ time.
However, what Karatsuba noted instead was that
$B = (x_0 + x_1)\cdot (y_0 + y_1) - A - C$
Boom: done.
Just one multiplication.}
that used about
$n^{\lg(3)}\approx n^{1.585}$ ticks.
At the next meeting 
Kolmogorov explained the result 
and closed the seminar.

This story illustrates a theme in the Theory of Computation:
perusing the Theory of Computation blog feed \autocite{blog:TOC}
shows that every day researchers
produce results saying, ``here is a job and here is a way to do it in less time,
or less space, etc.'' 
We are good at finding clever ways to do a job and thereby
reducing the upper bound
rate at which some computation resource, such as time,
is consumed while solving the problem.
But we are not good at proving nontrivial lower bounds, at saying
``no algorithm can do this job in less than this number of ticks.'' 
That's one reason why in the development below we will
mostly compare the growth rates of functions using a measure that is
about `less than or equal to'. 




\subsection{Motivation}
Suppose that we have a job to do, with two algorithms to do it.
When the input is size~$n\in\N$, the first algorithm
takes about $\sqrt{n}$ many ticks in the worst case,
while the second takes about $10\cdot \lg(n)$ ticks.\footnote{%
  Recall that $\lg(n)=\log_2(n)$ is defined by: start with~$n$ and then find
  the power of $2$
  that produces it.
  So, if $n=8$ then $\lg(n)=3$.
  If $n=10$ then $\lg(n)\approx 3.32$.}

For small $n$ the first algorithm's square root seems preferable to
the second's logarithm.
For instance, when the input is~$1000$
the two output values are $\sqrt{1000}\approx 31.62$
and $10 \lg(1000)\approx 99.66$.

\end{center}
However,
in the long run the values of $\sqrt{n}$ are much larger than
the values of $10\lg(n)$.
For instance, $\sqrt{1\,000\,000}=1\,000$ while
$10\lg(1\,000\,000)\approx 199.32$.

functions is more complicated than just
seeing which always has smaller output values.

\subsection{Definition}
We are studying functions that describe machine resource use.
These functions have natural number inputs
giving the size of the machine's inputs.
They often have natural number outputs but we 
need functions with real number outputs, such as~$\lg$.
The outputs are usually non-negative real numbers,
but a polynomial such as $n^2-5n+2$ has negative outputs for a few
small inputs and 
$\lg$ is not even defined 
for input~$0$.
So we will focus on functions 
$\map{f}{\N}{\R}$ that are \definend{eventually nonnegative}, where
there is an~$I\in\N$ such that $n>I$ implies that $f(n)$ is defined
and nonnegative.

\begin{definition}  \label{def:BigO}
  Let $g$ be a function that is eventually nonnegative.
  Then \definend{$\bigOh(g)$}, read ``big-O of $g$,''
  is the collection of eventually nonnegative functions~$f$
  satisfying this property:~there are numbers $N,C\in\N$ so that 
  whenever $n\geq N$ then $C\cdot g(n)\geq f(n)$. 
  We say that \definend{$f$ is in $\bigOh(g)$},
  that \definend{$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$},
  or that \definend{$f=\bigOh(g)$}.
\end{definition}

Think of `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' as meaning that
$f$'s growth rate is less than or equal to $g$'s rate.
These illustrate.
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo10.pdf}
  \hspace{3em}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo11.pdf}  
\end{center}
The sketch on the left has $g$ accelerating away
from~$f$ so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ since
its rate is smaller.
The sketch on the right is that $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ because
the rates are alike.

\begin{remark}
  \begin{parenum}
    \item
      We use the letter `O' because this is about the order of growth.
      % although the $\bigOh$~symbol only expresses an
      % upper bound.
    \item
      The `$f=\bigOh(g)$' notation is awkward but 
      standard.\footnotemark{} %
      The notation `$f\in\bigOh(g)$' is correct but not common.
    \item
      Some authors allow negative real outputs and write the
      inequality with absolute values, 
      $f(x)\leq C\cdot |g(x)|$.
    \item
      You often see constructions such as `$n^2+5n+6$ is $\bigOh(n^2)$',
      replacing the named functions $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=n^2$
      with expressions.
    \item
      Sometimes you see `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' stated as
      `$f(n)$ is $\bigOh(g(n))$'.
      Strictly speaking, this is
      wrong\Dash $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ are numbers, not functions, and big-O
      is a relationship between two functions.
  \end{parenum}
\end{remark}\footnotetext{%
  One way that it is awkward is that it does not follow the usual rules
  of equality.
  For one thing, you cannot rewrite it as `$\protect\bigOh(g)=f$'.
  Another way is that $n=\bigOh(n^2)$ and $n^2=\bigOh(n^2)$ does not
  allow us to conclude that $n=n^2$.}

\begin{example} \label{ex:BigOFromDefn}
  To directly apply the definition, given two functions we must produce
  suitable $N$ and~$C$, and verify that they work.
  Let $f(n)=n^2$ and $g(n)=n^3$.
  Then $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$, as $N=2$ and $C=1$ witness.
  The verification is that $n>2$ implies that $g(n)=n^3=n\cdot n^2$ is greater
  than $2\cdot n^2$, which is greater than $n^2=1\cdot f(n)$.

  Simiilarly, $f(n)=5n^2$ and $g(n)=n^4$ then to show $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$
  take $N=2$ and $C=2$.
  To verify:
  $n> 2$ implies that $C\cdot n^4=2\cdot n^2\cdot n^2\geq 8n^2> 5\cdot n^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The assertion that $f$ in~$\bigOh(g)$ does not mean that the graph of $g$ is
  eventually above the graph of~$f$.
  Consider $f(n)=n^2+1$ and~$g(n)=n^2$.
  The outputs of the second function
  are always less than those of the first, but $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$.
  To verify, take $N=2$ and~$C=2$.
  If $n>N$ we can write $n=j+2$ for $j\in\N^+$ and
  then $C\cdot g(n)=2n^2=2(j+2)^2=2j^2+8j+8$ is greater than
  $f(n)=(j+2)^2+1=j^2+4j+5$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The zero function~$Z$ is $\bigOh(g)$ for every
  eventually nonnegative~$g$.
  Just take $N=0$ and~$C=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Not every pair of eventually nonnegative functions is comparable.
For this function
\begin{equation*}
  f(n)=\begin{cases} 
         n^2  &\case{if $n$ is even}  \\
         n^4  &\case{if $n$ is odd}
       \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
$f$ is not~$\bigOh(n^3)$,
and $g(n)=n^3$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.

To see that $f$ is not~$\bigOh(n^3)$, observe that there are
arbitrarily large inputs~$n$ where $C=2$ does not suffice:
if $n$ is odd and $n>2$  then $f(n)=n^4=n\cdot n^3> 2\cdot n^3$.
Similar reasoning works for any~$C$.
The argument that $n^3$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$ goes the same way, on even inputs.
So the ordering imposed by $\bigOh{}$ on the set of
functions is only partial.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
  We often omit the technicality of `eventually nonnegative'.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic properties]  \label{lem:BigOAlgProps}
  Suppose that $f_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$ and that $f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_1)$.
\begin{parenum}
\item 
  For any positive constant $a\in\R^+$, the function $af_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$.
\item
  The sum $f_0+f_1$ is $\bigOh(g)$, where $g$ is defined by
  $g(n)=\max(g_0(n),g_1(n))$.  
  So if both $f_0$ and $f_1$ are $\bigOh(g)$ then $f_0+f_1$ is also $\bigOh(g)$.
\item
  The product $f_0f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_0g_1)$.
\end{parenum}
\end{lemma}


\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigOhReflexiveAndTransitive}
  The big-O relation is reflexive:~$f$ is $\bigOh(f)$.
  It is also transitive:~if $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and $g$ is~$\bigOh(h)$ then
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(h)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}
  Two eventually nonnegative functions have
  \definend{the same growth rate} if both $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and also
  $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$.
  We say \definend{$f$ is $\bigTheta(g)$} or, what is the same thing,
  $g$ is~$\bigTheta(f)$).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigThetaEquivalence}
  The relation between functions of having the same growth rate
  is an equivalence.
\end{lemma}


\begin{example}
  The order $\bigOh(n\cdot \lg(n))$ appears often.
  We will show that it equals $\bigOh(n!)$ by mutual containment of sets.
  
  Recall that logarithmic functions turn multiplication into addition:
  $\lg(a\cdot b)=\lg(a)+\lg(b)$.
  The $\bigOh(\lg(n!))\subseteq\bigOh(n\lg(n))$ direction follows from
  this.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots \lg(2) + \lg(1)            \\
            &< \lg(n) + \lg(n)   + \cdots + \lg(n)+\lg(n) = n\cdot\lg(n)
  \end{align*}
  The $\bigOh(n\lg(n))\subseteq\bigOh(\lg(n!))$ direction is
  trickier.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(2) + \lg(1)   \\
            &> \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2})   \\
            &> \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2}) \\
            &=(n/2)\cdot\lg(n/2)                      \\
            &=(n/2)\bigl[ \lg(n)-\lg(2) \bigr]
             =(1/2)\cdot n\lg(n)-(1/2)\cdot n\lg(2)
  \end{align*}
\end{example}


\begin{theorem}  \label{lem:LHBigO}
  Let $f,g$ be eventually nonegative and suppose that 
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup\set{\infty}$.
  \begin{parenum}
  \item If $L=\infty$ then $f$ grows faster than~$g$,
    meaning that $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$ but $f$ is not $\bigOh(g)$.
  \item If $L=0$ then $g$ grows faster than~$f$,
    so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
  \item If $L$ is strictly between $0$ and~$\infty$
    then the two functions have the same growth
    rate, that is, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.
  \end{parenum}
\end{theorem}

Directly applying the defintion of
$\bigOh$, as in \cref{ex:BigOFromDefn}, is sometimes the best way
to go.
But often
this result from first semester calculus about the limit of
a ratio of two functions makes the job easier.

\begin{theorem}[L'H\^{o}pital's Rule]
Suppose that $\map{f,g}{\R}{\R}$ are such that as $x\to\infty$ then both
$f(x)\to\infty$ and $g(x)\to\infty$.
If 
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f^\prime(x)/g^\prime(x)$ exists and equals
$L\in\R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$
then
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{theorem}

Taking the derivative requires functions with inputs that are real numbers,
not natural numbers,
so we need the following observation in order to apply
L'H\^opital's Rule to \cref{lem:LHBigO}.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns}
  Let $\map{F,G}{\R}{\R}$ and let $\map{f,g}{\N}{\leftclosed{0}{\infty}}$
  be such that
  for all natural numbers $F(n)=f(n)$ and $G(n)=g(n)$.
  If $\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)/G(x)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup{\set{\infty}}$
  then $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{example}
  For polynomial functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule works well.
  Consider again $\fcn{f,g}{\N}{\N}$ given by 
  $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=2n^2+3n+4$.
  On the associated real functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule gives this. 
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2+3x+4}{x^2+5x+6}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x+3}{2x+5}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4}{2}
    =2
  \end{equation*}
  By \cref{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns} the limit of the natural number functions
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ is also~$2$.
  With that, \cref{lem:LHBigO} says that the two
  have the same growth rate, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.

  Similarly, for $f(n)=2n^2+3n+4$ and $g(n)=n^3+5n+6$,
  L'H\^opital's Rule shows that
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^3+5x+6}{2x^2+3x+4}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+5x+6}{4x+3}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{6x}{4}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  and so $f$ grows at a rate strictly slower than~$g$, that is,
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  Non-polynomial functions also come up often.
  Compare $f(n)=n^2$ with $g(x)=2^n$,
  convert to real functions and apply L'H\^{o}pital's Rule.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x}{x^2}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot\ln(2)}{2x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot(\ln(2))^2}{2}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  Thus, by \cref{lem:LHBigO},
  $f$'s order is strictly less than $g$'s\Dash $f$ is in~$\bigOh(g)$ but
  $g$ is not in~$\bigOh(f)$.

  The same approach also shows that the order of $f(x)=\lg(x)$ is 
  strictly less than the order of $g(x)=x$.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\lg(x)}{x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)\cdot(1/\ln(2))}{1}
    =0
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}

Big-O is only a rough metric in practice even if we limit ourselves
to subexponential functions in part because 
leading coefficients play a role.
Consider algorithms that take time give by the quadratic functions $cn^2$.
An enormous coefficient such as $c=2^{100}$ would make such an algorithm
just as impractical as one that takes exponential time~$2^n$.
(Although, often when an algorithm is discovered that has a
very large leading coefficient then soon researchers develop
improvements that lower the coefficient to a usable level.
In the same way, while in the past we have sometimes seen
algorithms that are polynomial but with 
large powers, such as $n^{10}$, often these have fallen
to small powers as researchers sharpen the approach.)

We close by remarking that the machines on which 
algorithms are implemented will vary widely,
for instance in the time that they take to write to memory.
Thus, without specifying the exact implementation and
the machine, necessarily means that any measure will be rough,
so the face that big-O is a rough measure seems less of a
disadvantage.

\section{Time complexity}
We are interested in classifying various problems: for which problems
are there relatively good algorithms, and for which problems are there not?
Given a problem we will be interested in its best solution.

\begin{definition}
Let $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$.
A language~$\lang{L}$ is an element of \definend{$\DTIME(t)$}
if there is a deterministic
Turing machine that decides~$\lang{L}$ and runs in time~$\bigOh(t)$, and
that language is an element of \definend{$\DTIME(t)$}
if there is a nondeterministic
Turing machine that decides~$\lang{L}$ and runs in time~$\bigOh(t)$. 
\end{definition}

This does not say that an algorithm is in that class,
rather it says that a language is in that class.
A language is in that class if there is an algorithm for its decision problem
that has the stated big-O runtime behavior.

\begin{definition}
A language is in 
the complexity class \definend{polynomial time $\compclass{P}$},
often read \definend{polytime},
if it is a member of $\DTIME(n^c)$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{equation*}
  \compclass{P} =\bigcup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(n^c)          
     =\DTIME(n)\cup\DTIME(n^2)\cup\DTIME(n^3)\cup\,\cdots
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
A language is in 
the complexity class \definend{exponential time $\EXP$}
if it is a member of $\DTIME(2^{n^c})$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{equation*}
  \EXP =\bigcup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(2^{n^c})           
       =\DTIME(2^n)\cup\DTIME(2^{n^2})\cup\DTIME(2^{n^3})\cup\,\cdots
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

As discussed in the prior subsection,
polytime is our first take at can-conceivably-be-used,
and we say that the problem is `tractable'. 
in contrast, $\EXP$ as too slow; we can't compute it.
We say it is `intractable'.
This view of the matter began with J~Edmunds.

\begin{remark}
A natural concern is that if we change the computing model 
then we get different results.
What if a problem is $\DTIME(n^3)$ for a 
two-tape Turing machine but $\DTIME(n^5)$ for a one-tape 
machine?.

Experience with a wide range of alternative computational models is that
in almost all cases a Turing machine can be simulated on such a different model
machine in time~$\bigOh(n^2)$, and in any event can  be done
in polytime.
So if we have polytime sloppiness built in then we can essentially
ignore the model issue. 
\end{remark}









\section{Complexity classes}

 
\begin{definition}[the complexity class $\compclass{NP}$]
A language $\lang{L}$ is a member of 
$\compclass{NP}$ if there is a polynomial $\map{p}{\N}{\N}$ and a
polynomial time Turing machine $\TM$, the \definend{verifier}
for $\lang{L}$, such that 
\begin{equation*}
  \forall \sigma\in\strings 
  \big[ \sigma\in\lang{L} 
     \,\Longleftrightarrow\, 
     \exists w\in \set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}(\TM(\seq{\sigma,w})=1)  \big]
\end{equation*}
($w$ is the \definend{witness} or \definend{certificate} for $\sigma$).
\end{definition}

Note that the witness is restricted in length so that the verifier,
which as a Turing machine reads one bit at a time, can 
read it through in polynomial time.
Note also that we can take the polynomial~$p$ to be trivial $p(i)=0$
to get that $\compclass{P}\subseteq\compclass{NP}$.

\begin{example}
The satisfiability problem is in~$\compclass{NP}$.
That is, $\lang{L}\in\compclass{NP}$ where this is $\lang{L}$.
\begin{equation*}
  \lang{L}=\set{\varphi\suchthat 
             \text{$\varphi$ is a satisfiable propositional logic formula}}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Discussion}

These two  
computational problems seem similar.

\begin{example}
Formally, a mathematical proof as a sequence of statements,
$\sigma_0$, $\sigma_1$, \ldots, $\sigma_k$,
where the final statement is the conclusion. 
Each statement either is itself an axiom or
else follows from the statements before it
by an application
of one of the agreed-upon derivation rules.
From experience we know that finding the proof of a desired  
conclusion can be very hard; often it seems to take a leap of insight.
Trying to brute-force it by taking all combinations of 
all the axioms and all the derivation rules would be very slow.
In contrast, if we were given the 
sequence of statements along with a description
of each step's rules, and asked only to check the 
sequence for correctness,
then that would be easy and quick.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider a complicated propositional logic expression, say
$(\neg(P_1\vee (P_2\wedge P_3)\rightarrow \neg P_4)\leftrightarrow (P_2\rightarrow P_3)\rightarrow (P_1\wedge \neg P_4)$.
We want to find whether it is \definend{satisfiable}, 
whether there is any assignment of 
truth values $\true$ or~$\false$ to the 
variables $P_1,\idots, P_4$ that makes the statement
as a whole have the value $\true$.
We can imagine trying to answer by reasoning
in some way as, 
``Well, the formula has an if and only if arrow so I can see that
it boils down to \ldots''
but an algorithm that unfailingly finds the solution that way is not evident.
Alternatively, 
you could go through the possible assignments
$\seq{\false, \false, \false, \false}$, 
then $\seq{\false, \false, \false, \true}$, etc., 
until one causes the statement to yield $\true$, if one ever does.
However, if instead you were given an answer, an assignment to the variables,
and only asked to verify that it solves the problem, then you 
could do that quickly.
\end{example}

These seem alike in that, putting aside brute force,
we feel that for each finding an answer is hard and somehow involves creativity.
But if we are simply handed a solution then verifying that it is right 
calls only for a dull calculation.

That is, there are problems where finding an answer is quick and easy.
The two problems above seem different\Dash they \emph{feel} hard to solve, 
although
they have the property that once an answer is given then it is easy
to check.
Despite this very strong feeling, though, we are today unable
to prove that they are of different hardnesses.
This question of whether we can tell the two apart
is certainly the sexiest one in Computing today.
One reason it is attractive is that many pressing problems
have been shown to be like the proof-generating or satisfiability questions:
they don't appear to be solvable efficiently
their solutions can be verified quickly.
however, we cannot today prove that  
that is the case.

\begin{frame}{Polynomial time reducible}
\dfn

\begin{frame}{$\DTIME$}
We are interested in classifying various problems: for which problems
are there relatively good algorithms, and for which problems are there not?
Given a problem we will be interested in its best solution.

\pause
\dfn
Let $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$.
A language is an element of \alert{$\DTIME(t)$} if there is a 
Turing machine that decides the language and runs in time~$\bigo(t)$. 

This does not say that an algorithm is in that class,
rather it says that a language is in that class.
A language is in that class if there is an algorithm for its decision problem
that has the stated big-oh time behavior.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Polynomial time}
\dfn
A language is in 
the complexity class \alert{polynomial time} \alert{$\P$}
(often read \textit{polytime})
if it is a member of $\DTIME(n^c)$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{align*}
  \P &=\cup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(n^c)           \\
     &=\DTIME(n)\cup\DTIME(n^2)\cup\DTIME(n^3)\cup\,\cdots
\end{align*}

We take polytime as a first take at can-conceivably-be-run.
People say the problem is \alert{tractable}.

\rem
A natural concern is that if we change the computing model 
then we get different results.
We can imagine a situation where a problem is $\DTIME(n^3)$ for a 
two-tape Turing machine, and a $\DTIME(n^5)$ for a one-tape 
machine, or for Post's computation model.

Experience with a wide range of alternative computational models is that
in almost all cases a Turing machine can be simulated on such a different model
machine in time~$\bigo(n^2)$, and in any event can  be done
in polytime.
So if we have polytime sloppiness built in then we can essentially
ignore the model issue. 
\end{frame}

\section{P=NP}


\begin{frame}{$\NTIME$, $\NP$}
The satisfiability problems lends itself to the unbounded 
parallelism of a nondeterministic machine.

\dfn
Let $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$.
A language is an element of \alert{$\NTIME(t)$} if there is a 
nondeterministic Turing machine that decides the language and runs in 
time~$\bigo(t)$. 

\pause
\dfn
A language is a member of the complexity class 
\alert{nondeterministic polynomial time $\NP$}
if it is a member of $\NTIME(n^c)$ for some power~$c$.
\begin{equation*}
  \NP=\NTIME(n)\cup\NTIME(n^2)\cup\NTIME(n^3)\cup\cdots
\end{equation*}

\exm  
The satisfiability problem is in~$\NP$.
That is, $\lang\in\NP$ where this is $\lang$.
\begin{equation*}
  \lang=\set{\varphi\suchthat 
             \text{$\varphi$ is a satisfiable propositional logic formula}}
\end{equation*}

\end{frame}




\begin{frame}{Reduction of one problem to another}
\exm 
Consider this problem.

\begin{problemstatement}{Graph Colorability}
Given a graph and a natural number bound~$b$.
Decide if the graph is \alert{$b$-colorable}, that is,
whether its vertices can be partitioned into $b$-many classes 
$\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{C}_0\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{C}_{b-1}$ such
that never are two same-class vertices 
connected by an edge.
\end{problemstatement}

We will show that a quick way to solve the 
Satisfiability problem would give us a quick way to solve the 
Graph Colorability problem.
That is, 
$\text{Graph Colorability}\leq\text{Satisfiability}$. 
\pause
We will illustrate with $3$-colorability.
\begin{center}
  \only<1,2>{\includegraphics{asy/peterson_graph.pdf}}
  \only<3->{\includegraphics{asy/peterson_graph1.pdf}}
\end{center}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
We will produce a set of propositional logic clauses 
where the set is satisfiable\Dash 
there is a set of boolean values for the variables making  all the clauses in
the set true\Dash if and only if the graph has a $3$-coloring.  

\pause
Suppose that the graph has $n$~vertices $\mathcal{V}=\set{v_0\ldots\,,v_{n-1}}$.
The boolean variables for the propositional logic clauses
will come in three types
\begin{equation*}
  {\color{red} r_0,\ldots,r_{n-1}}
  \qquad
  {\color{blue}b_0,\ldots,b_{n-1}}
  \qquad
  {\color{green}g_0,\ldots,g_{n-1}}
\end{equation*}
where {\color{red}$r_i$} will be $T$ if vertex~$i$ is colored red, etc.
There will be two types of clauses.
First, for each vertex~$v_i$ there is a clause ensuring that it gets a color.
\begin{equation*}
  ({\color{red}r_i}\vee{\color{blue}b_i}\vee{\color{green}g_i})
\end{equation*}
Second, for each edge $\set{v_i,v_j}$ there are three clauses that 
together ensure that the edge
does not connect two same-color vertices.
\begin{equation*}
  ({\color{red} \neg r_i\wedge \neg r_j}),
  \qquad
  ({\color{blue} \neg b_i\wedge \neg b_j}),
  \qquad
  ({\color{green} \neg g_i\wedge \neg g_j})
\end{equation*}
\end{frame}




\begin{frame}
\exm 
Consider this problem.

\begin{problemstatement}{Clique}
Given a graph and a natural number bound~$b$.
Decide if the graph has a $b$-vertex \alert{clique},
a set of vertices such that every two are connected by an edge.
\end{problemstatement}

We will show that a quick way to solve the Clique problem would
give us a quick way to solve the Satisfiability problem, so that
$\text{Satisfiability}\leq\text{Clique}$. 

\pause
Given a set of propositional logic clauses, 
\begin{equation*}
  \set{x_0\wedge \neg x_1,\, x_0,\, \neg(\neg x_0\wedge \neg x_1)}
\end{equation*}
we can make a single statement by
AND-ing them together, 
\begin{equation*}
  (x_0\wedge \neg x_1)\wedge(x_0)\wedge(\neg(\neg x_0\wedge \neg x_1))
\end{equation*}
and put that
statement into 
Conjunctive Normal Form.
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi=(x_0\vee x_1)\wedge(\neg x_0\vee\neg x_1)\wedge(x_0\vee \neg x_1)
\end{equation*}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
The statement
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi=(x_0\vee x_1)\wedge(\neg x_0\vee\neg x_1)\wedge(x_0\vee \neg x_1)
\end{equation*}
has three clauses.
\begin{equation*}
  C_0=x_0\vee x_1
  \qquad
  C_1=\neg x_0\vee\neg x_1
  \qquad
  C_2=x_0\vee \neg x_1
\end{equation*}
We will construct a graph~$\mathcal{G}$ that has a $k$-clique if and only if the
statement~$\varphi$ is satisfiable ($k$ will be the number of clauses, $3$).

\pause
Put a vertex in $\mathcal{G}$ for each occurrence of a variable in a clause.
our example has two variables in each of three clauses.
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{V} &=\{v_{0,0},v_{0,1}, \\
              &\qquad v_{1,0},v_{1,1}, \\
              &\qquad v_{2,0},v_{2,0}\}
\end{align*}  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
Recall the clauses.
\begin{equation*}
  C_0=x_0\vee x_1
  \qquad
  C_1=\neg x_0\vee\neg x_1
  \qquad
  C_2=x_0\vee \neg x_1
\end{equation*}
Put an edge in~$\mathcal{G}$ if the associated variables~$x_i$ 
are in different clauses
and not complementary (i.e., not $x_0$ and $\neg x_0$).
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{V} &=\set{v_{0,0},v_{0,1},v_{1,0},v_{1,1},v_{2,0},v_{2,1}} \\
  \mathcal{E} &=\{\,\set{v_{0,0},v_{2,0}},\,
                  \set{v_{0,0},v_{1,1}},\,
                  \set{v_{0,0},v_{2,1}},  \\
              &\qquad \set{v_{0,1},v_{1,0}},\,
                      \set{v_{0,1},v_{2,0}}, \\
              &\qquad \set{v_{1,0},v_{2,0}},\,
                      \set{v_{1,0},v_{2,1}}, \\
              &\qquad \set{v_{1,1},v_{2,0}}\,\}
\end{align*}

Now the statement~$\varphi$ is satisfiable if and only if there is a 
$3$-clique  in the graph.
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi={\color{red}(x_0\vee x_1)}
          \wedge{\color{blue}(\neg x_0\vee\neg x_1)}
          \wedge{\color{green}(x_0\vee \neg x_1)}

\begin{frame}{Polynomial time reducible}
\dfn
Consider two languages $\lang,\hat{\lang}\subset\ks{\set{0,1}}$
Then $\lang$ is \alert{polytime reducible} to $\hat{\lang}$,
written $\lang\leq_p\hat{\lang}$ if there is a 
polytime computable function $\map{f}{\ks{\set{0,1}}}{\ks{\set{0,1}}}$
such that for every string $\sigma\in\ks{\set{0,1}}$ we have
$\sigma\in\lang$ if and only if $f(\sigma)\in\hat{\lang}$.

This ranks problems (actually, languages) by hardness.
A quick way to solve~$\hat{\lang}$
woud give a quick way to solve~$\lang$.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{$\protect\NP$-hard, $\protect\NP$-complete}
\dfn
We say that $\hat{\lang}$ is \alert{$\NP$-hard} 
if $\lang\leq_p\hat{\lang}$ for every
$\lang\in\NP$.
We say that $\hat{\lang}$ is \alert{$\NP$-complete} if 
$\hat{\lang}$ is $\NP$-hard and $\hat{\lang}\in\NP$.
\end{frame}



\begin{frame}{Alternate characterization of $\NP$}
\lem
A language~$\lang$ is a member of~$\NP$ if and only if there is
a polytime deterministic Turing machine~$\mach$, 
and a polynomial~$p$, such that for all strings $\sigma\in\ks{\set{0,1}}$
the following holds.
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma\in\lang
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \exists w(\sigma)\in\set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}\lbrack\mach(\sigma,w(\sigma))=1\rbrack
  \tag{$*$}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip
The string~$w(\sigma)$ is a \alert{witness} or \alert{certificate} for~$\sigma$.
Note that a single $\mach$ works for all~$\sigma$.

\pf
Suppose first that $\lang\in\NP$.
Then it is decided by some nondeterministic machine~$\mach$ whose running time
is a polynomial~$p$.
For any input $\sigma\in\lang$ there is a sequence of nondeterministic forks
in the computation history leading to acceptance by~$\mach$,
and this sequence~$w$ can serve as the witness
for~$\sigma$.
Note that $w$ has length $p(|\sigma|)$ and can be verified in polynomial
time by a deterministic machine simulating $\mach$, and
thus $\lang\in\NP$.\continued
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
For the other direction 
suppose that ($*$) holds; we will describe a polytime NDTM that 
decides~$\lang$.
The idea is that this NDTM tests every possible witness
$w\in\set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}$, using the polytime 
$\mach(\sigma,w)$ to do so.
Because of the unbounded parallelism, the total time taken
is polytime. 
Because of the case assumption that ($*$) holds,
$\sigma\in\lang$ if and only if
the NDTM will find such a witness $w(\sigma)$. 
\qed
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Some $\protect\NP$-hard problems}
We won't give proofs of these; see Garey and~Johnson's book.

\begin{problemstatement}{Factoring}
Given a number $n\in\N$ and lower and upper bounds $b_L,b_U\in\N$.
Decide if the number has a prime factor in the interval.
\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the prime.
(We don't know if this problem is $\NP$-complete.)

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Travelling salesman}
Given a set of cities~$C=\set{c_0,\ldots,c_{n-1}}$,
a function $\map{d}{C\times C}{\N}$ 
giving the distance between any two cities,
and a natural number~$k$.
Decide if there is a tour of all the cities 
$\sequence{c_{i_0},\ldots,c_{i_{n-1}}}$
whose total distance is less than~$k$.
\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the circuit.
(This problem is known to be $\NP$-complete.)

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Crossword puzzle}
Given a set of $2n$-many words.
Decide if they can be arranged into an $n\times n$ array 
crossword.
\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the array.
(This problem is known to be $\NP$-complete.)
\end{frame}

\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the subset of numbers.
(This problem is known to be $\NP$-complete.)

\begin{frame}{The canonical six $\protect\NP$ complete problems}
We have already seen this $\NP$-complete problem.

\begin{problemstatement}{Satisfiability}
Given a set of propositional logic statements $\varphi_j$. 
Decide if there is 
an assignment of booleans $\set{\textsc{T},\textsc{F}}$ to the
variables making all the $\varphi_j$ true.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
This variation
is sometimes convenient and is also $\NP$-complete.

\begin{problemstatement}{Three-satisfiability ($3$-SAT)}
Given a set of propositional logic statements $\varphi_j$, 
each with no more than three variables. 
Decide if there is  
an assignment of booleans $\set{\textsc{T},\textsc{F}}$ to the
variables making all the $\varphi_j$ true.   
\end{problemstatement}

\rem Two-satisfiability is not $\NP$-complete.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
\begin{problemstatement}{Vertex cover}
Given a graph and a natural number bound~$b$. 
Decide if
there is a size~$b$ \alert{vertex cover}, a
subset of the vertices~$C$ such that for every edge
$\sequence{v,\hat{v}}$ an endpoint 
is in~$C$.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Clique}
Given a graph and a natural number bound~$b$.
Decide if the graph has a $b$-vertex \alert{clique},
a set of vertices such that every two are connected by an edge.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Hamiltonian circuit}
Given a graph. 
Decide if it has a \alert{Hamiltonian circuit},
an ordering of all the vertices
$\sequence{v_0,v_1\ldots,v_{n-1}}$ such that 
every pair $v_i$ and~$v_{i+1}$
are connected by an edge, and also connected are $v_{n-1}$ and~$v_0$.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Three dimensional matching ($3$-DM)}
Given three disjoint finite sets 
$X$, $Y$, and~$Z$, and some $T\subseteq X\times Y\times Z$
(so $T$ consists of triples $\sequence{x,y,z}\in T$).
Decide if $T$ has a \alert{matching subset} $M\subseteq T$:~if 
$\sequence{x_0,y_0,z_0},\sequence{x_1,y_1,z_1} \in M$ then
$x_0\neq x_1$ and $y_0\neq y_1$ and~$z_0\neq z_1$. 
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Partition}
Given a multiset~$S\subset\N$ 
(a collection of natural numbers in which there may be repeats).
Decide if~$S$ can be \alert{partitioned} into 
two disjoint sub-multisets $S_0$ and~$S_1$ such that
the sum of the members of~$S_0$ equals the sum of the members of~$S_1$.
\end{problemstatement}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$P=NP$ guesses?}
The $\P=\NP$ question is one of the 
\href{http://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems}{Millennium Problems},
for which there is a $\$1,000,000$~reward.

\pause
Most experts believe that $\P\neq\NP$.
Arora and Barak put it this way.
\begin{quotation}\small
\noindent  
If you have ever eattempted a crossword puzzle, you konw that it is much 
harder to solve it from scratch than to verify a solution provided by 
someone else.
Likewise, solving a math homework problem  by yourself is usually much 
harder than reading and understanding a solution provided by your instructor.
The usual explanation for this difference of effort is that finding a solution 
\ldots\ 
requires \emph{creative effort}.
Verifying a solution is much easier since somebody else has already done
the cretaive part.
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
Scott Aaronson in 
\href{http://www.scottaaronson.com/blog/?p=122}{a 2006 blog post}
(and also a
\href{http://www.scottaaronson.com/blog/?p=1720}{2014 blog post})
gives many reasons: here are a few.
\begin{desc}
\item[The Obvious Argument.] After half a century, we still don't 
know any algorithm for an $\NP$-complete problem that runs in 
subexponential time.
\ldots
In math, if decades of research fail to turn up an object, 
and there's no a priori reason to suppose the object exists, 
it's usually a good strategy to conjecture that the object doesn't exist. 
\item[The Empirical Argument.] \ldots\ people certainly want fast ways 
to solve $\NP$-complete problems in practice, 
and they haven't been able to invent them.
\item[The Philosophical Argument.] If $\P=\NP$, then the world would be a 
profoundly different place than we usually assume it to be. 
There would be no special value in ``creative leaps,'' 
no fundamental gap between solving a problem and recognizing the solution 
once it's found. 
Everyone who could appreciate a symphony would be Mozart; 
everyone who could follow a step-by-step argument would be Gauss; 
everyone who could recognize a good investment strategy would be 
Warren Buffett.
\end{desc}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
Knuth has the opposite guess.

\begin{quotation} \small
I've come to believe that $\P = \NP$, \ldots\, 
Some of my reasoning is admittedly na\"ive: It's hard to believe that 
$\P\neq\NP$ and that so many brilliant people have failed to discover why.
\ldots

My main point, however, is that I don't believe that the equality 
$\P = \NP$ will turn out to be helpful even if it is proved, 
because such a proof will almost surely be nonconstructive. 
\ldots\ 
Mathematics is full of examples where something is proved to exist, 
yet the proof tells us nothing about how to find it. 
Knowledge of the mere existence of an algorithm is completely different 
from the knowledge of an actual algorithm.

More to the point, Robertson and Seymour have proved a famous theorem in graph 
theory: Any class of graphs that is closed under taking minors has a 
finite number of minor-minimal graphs. 
% (A minor of a graph is any graph obtainable by deleting vertices, deleting edges, or shrinking edges to a point. A minor-minimal graph H for is a graph whose smaller minors all belong to although H itself doesn't.) 
Therefore there exists a polynomial-time algorithm to decide whether or not 
a given graph belongs to that class: The algorithm checks that G doesn't 
contain any of the class's minor-minimal graphs as a minor.
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
\begin{quotation}\small
But we don't know what that algorithm is, except for a few special classes, 
because the set of minor-minimal graphs is often unknown. 
The algorithm exists, but it's not known to be discoverable in finite time.

% This consequence of Robertson and Seymour's theorem definitely surprised me, when I learned about it while reading a paper by LovÃ¡sz. And it tipped the balance, in my mind, toward the hypothesis that P = N P.

The moral is that people should distinguish between known (or knowable) 
polynomial-time algorithms and arbitrary polynomial-time algorithms. 
People might never be able to implement a polynomial-time-worst-case algorithm 
for satisfiability, even though $\P$ happens to equal $\NP$.
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}
