We will review the basics of the orders of growth of functions.
Because this is a review, we will not give a detailed development, just enough for our needs.
And we will leave the proofs for the exercises.

\section{Definition}
For a job there may be many algorithms.
We may want to choose one that is faster,
or consumes less of some other resource such as memory.
That is, algorithms take time that 
is a function of the size of their input and
we have tools to classify their behavior.

First, an anecdote.
Here is the grade school multiplication algorithm.
% How long does it take?
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{array}{@{}r@{}}
    678\\
    \times 42\\  \hline
    1356\\
   +27120\\  \hline
   28476
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
For each digit of the multiplier~$42$ we 
multiply each digit of the multiplicand~$678$.
That's a nested loop.
The natural guess is that multiplication of two $n$-bit numbers
requires a time of about $n^2$ ticks.

% https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Andrej_Nikolajewitsch_Kolmogorov.jpg
\margingraphic{0.20\textwidth}{complexity/Kolmogorov.jpg}{\label{fig:kolmogorov}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov}{Andrey Kolmogorov 1903--1987}}
In 1960 A~Kolmogorov organized a seminar at Moscow State University with the
goal of proving this.
Within a week one of his students,
\citetext{A~Karatsuba}{See \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Karatsuba}.},
had found that it is false.
Karatsuba produced a 
\citetext{clever algorithm}{%
The idea is to let $k = \ceiling{n/2}$ 
and write $x = x_1 2^k + x_0$ and $y = y_1 2^k + y_0$
(so $678=21 \cdot 2^5+6$ and $42=1\cdot 2^5+10$). 
Then we have $xy = A\cdot 2^{2k} + B\cdot 2^k + C$ 
where $A = x_1 y_1$, and $B = x_1 y_0 + x_0 y_1$, and~$C = x_0 y_0$
(here, $28\,476=21\cdot 2^{10}+216\cdot 2^5+60$). 
The multiplications by $2^{2k}$ and $2^{k}$ are just bit-shifts to known 
locations independent of the values of $x$ and $y$, 
so they don't affect the time much. 
But the two multiplications for 
$B$ seem remove all the advantage and still give $\Theta(n^2)$ time.
However, what Karatsuba noted instead was that
$B = (x_0 + x_1)\cdot (y_0 + y_1) - A - C$
Boom: done.
Just one multiplication.}
that used about
$n^{\lg(3)}\approx n^{1.585}$ ticks.
At the next meeting 
Kolmogorov explained the beautiful result 
and terminated the seminar.

This story illustrates a theme in the Theory of Computation:
we are good at giving upper bounds for the resources used by
algorithms, at finding increasingly clever ways to do a job.
Perusing the blog feed \autocite{blog:TOC}
shows that every day researchers
produce results saying, ``here is a job and here is a way to do it faster, or in
less space, etc., than we used to do it.'' 
But we seem not at all good at proving lower bounds, at saying
``no algorithm can do this job in less than this number of ticks.'' 
That's one reason why in the development below we will
mostly compare the growth rates of functions using a
`less than or equal to' measure. 




\subsection{Motivation}
To determine if a number~$n\in\N$ is prime, two of the methods available are 
the sieve and the Fermat test.
The sieve takes about $\sqrt{n}$ many ticks,
while the Fermat test takes about $10\cdot \lg(n)$ ticks.\footnote{%
  Recall that $\lg(n)=\log_2(n)$ is defined by: start with~$n$ and then find
  the power of $2$
  that produces it.
  So, if $n=8$ then $\lg(n)=3$.
  If $n=10$ then $\lg(n)\approx 3.32$.}

At first the sieve's square root function seems preferable to
the Fermat test's logarithmic function.
For instance, when the input is~$1000$
the two output values are $\sqrt{1000}\approx 31.62$
and $10\cdot\lg(1000)\approx 99.66$.

\end{center}
However,
in the long run the values of $\sqrt{n}$ are much larger than
the values of $10\lg(n)$.
For instance, $\sqrt{1\,000\,000}=1\,000$ while
$10\lg(1\,000\,000)\approx 199.32$.

\end{center}

functions is more complicated than just
seeing which always has smaller output values.

We are studying functions that describe machine resource use.
These functions have natural number inputs
giving the size of the machine's inputs.
They often have natural number outputs but we 
need functions with real number outputs, such as~$\lg$.
The outputs are usually non-negative real numbers,
but a polynomial such as $n^2-5n+2$ has negative outputs for a few
small inputs and 
$\lg$ is not even defined 
for input~$0$.
So we will focus on functions 
$\map{f}{\N}{\R}$ that are \definend{eventually nonnegative}, where
there is an~$I\in\N$ such that $n>I$ implies that $f(n)$ is defined
and nonnegative.

\begin{definition}  \label{def:BigO}
  Let $g$ be a function that is eventually nonnegative.
  Then \definend{$\bigOh(g)$}, read ``big-O of $g$,''
  is the collection of eventually nonnegative functions~$f$
  satisfying this property:~there are numbers $N,C\in\N$ so that 
  whenever $n\geq N$ then $C\cdot g(n)\geq f(n)$. 
  We say \definend{$f$ is in $\bigOh(g)$}, \definend{$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$},
  or \definend{$f=\bigOh(g)$}.
\end{definition}

Think of `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' as meaning that
$f$'s growth rate is less than or equal to $g$'s rate.
These illustrate.
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo10.pdf}
  \hspace{3em}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo11.pdf}  
\end{center}
On the left $g$ accelerates away from~$f$ so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ in that
its rate is smaller.
On the right $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ because
the rates are alike.

\begin{remark}
  \begin{parenum}
    \item
      The `$f=\bigOh(g)$' notation is awkward but 
      standard.\footnotemark{} %
      The notation `$f\in\bigOh(g)$' is correct but not common.
    \item
      We use `O' because this is about the order of growth.
      % although the $\bigOh$~symbol only expresses an
      % upper bound.
    \item
      Some authors allow negative real outputs and write the
      inequality with absolute values, 
      $f(x)\leq C\cdot |g(x)|$.
    \item
      You often see constructions such as `$n^2+5n+6$ is $\bigOh(n^2)$',
      replacing the named functions $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=n^2$
      with expressions.
    \item
      Sometimes you see `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' stated as
      `$f(n)$ is $\bigOh(g(n))$'.
      This is
      wrong because $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ are numbers, not functions, and big-O
      relates functions.
  \end{parenum}
\end{remark}\footnotetext{%
  One way that it is awkward is that it does not follow the usual rules
  of equality.
  For one thing, you cannot rewrite it as `$\protect\bigOh(g)=f$'.
  Another is that $n=\bigOh(n^2)$ and $n^2=\bigOh(n^2)$ does not
  allow us to conclude $n=n^2$.}

\begin{example} \label{ex:BigOFromDefn}
  To directly apply the definition, given two functions we must produce
  suitable $N$ and~$C$, and verify that they work.
  Thus, if $f(n)=n^2$ and $g(n)=n^3$ then $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$   
  as $N=2$ and $C=1$ witness.
  The verification is that $n>2$ implies that $g(n)=n^3=n\cdot n^2$ is greater
  than $2\cdot n^2$, which is greater than $n^2=1\cdot f(n)$.

  If $f(n)=5n^2$ and $g(n)=n^4$ then to show $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$
  take $N=2$ and $C=2$.
  To verify:
  $n> 2$ implies that $C\cdot n^4=2\cdot n^2\cdot n^2\geq 8n^2> 5\cdot n^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The assertion that $f$ in~$\bigOh(g)$ does not mean that the graph of $g$ is
  eventually above the graph of~$f$.
  Consider $f(n)=n^2+1$ and~$g(n)=n^2$.
  The outputs of the second function
  are always less than those of the first, but $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$.
  To verify, take $N=2$ and~$C=2$.
  If $n>N$ we can write $n=j+2$ for $j\in\N^+$ and
  then $C\cdot g(n)=2n^2=2(j+2)^2=2j^2+8j+8$ is greater than
  $f(n)=(j+2)^2+1=j^2+4j+5$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The zero function~$Z$ is $\bigOh(g)$ for every
  eventually nonnegative~$g$.
  Just take $N=0$ and~$C=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Not every pair of eventually nonnegative functions is comparable.
For this function
\begin{equation*}
  f(n)=\begin{cases} 
         n^2  &\case{if $n$ is even}  \\
         n^4  &\case{if $n$ is odd}
       \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
$f$ is not~$\bigOh(n^3)$,
and $n^3$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
To see that $f$ is not~$\bigOh(n^3)$, observe that there are
arbitrarily large inputs~$n$ where $C=2$ does not suffice:
if $n$ is odd and $n>2$  then $f(n)=n^4=n\cdot n^3> 2\cdot n^3$.
Similar reasoning works for any~$C$.
The argument that $n^3$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$ goes the same way, on even inputs.
So the ordering imposed by $\bigOh{}$ on the set of
functions is only partial.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
  We often omit the `eventually nonnegative' technicality.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic properties]  \label{lem:BigOAlgProps}
  Suppose that $f_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$ and that $f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_1)$.
\begin{parenum}
\item 
  For any positive constant $a\in\R^+$, the function $af_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$.
\item
  The sum $f_0+f_1$ is $\bigOh(g)$, where $g$ is defined by
  $g(n)=\max(g_0(n),g_1(n))$.  
  So if both $f_0$ and $f_1$ are $\bigOh(g)$ then $f_0+f_1$ is also $\bigOh(g)$.
\item
  The product $f_0f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_0g_1)$.
\end{parenum}
\end{lemma}


\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigOhReflexiveAndTransitive}
  The big-O relation is reflexive:~$f$ is $\bigOh(f)$.
  It is also transitive:~if $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and $g$ is~$\bigOh(h)$ then
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(h)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}
  Two eventually nonnegative functions have
  \definend{the same growth rate} if both $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and also
  $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$.
  We say \definend{$f$ is $\bigTheta(g)$} or, what is the same thing,
  $g$ is~$\bigTheta(f)$).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigThetaEquivalence}
  The relation between functions of having the same growth rate
  is an equivalence.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}  \label{lem:LHBigO}
  Let $f,g$ be eventually nonegative and suppose that 
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup\set{\infty}$.
  \begin{parenum}
  \item If $L=\infty$ then $f$ grows faster than~$g$,
    meaning that $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$ but $f$ is not $\bigOh(g)$.
  \item If $L=0$ then $g$ grows faster than~$f$,
    so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
  \item If $L$ is strictly between $0$ and~$\infty$
    then the two functions have the same growth
    rate, that is, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.
  \end{parenum}
\end{theorem}

Directly applying the defintion of
$\bigOh$, as in \cref{ex:BigOFromDefn}, is sometimes the best way
to go.
But often
this result from first semester calculus about the limit of
a ratio of two functions makes the job easier.

\begin{theorem}[L'H\^{o}pital's Rule]
Suppose that $\map{f,g}{\R}{\R}$ are such that as $x\to\infty$ then both
$f(x)\to\infty$ and $g(x)\to\infty$.
If 
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f^\prime(x)/g^\prime(x)$ exists and equals
$L\in\R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$
then
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{theorem}

Taking the derivative requires functions with inputs that are real numbers,
not natural numbers,
so we need the following observation in order to apply
L'H\^opital's Rule to \cref{lem:LHBigO}.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns}
  Let $\map{F,G}{\R}{\R}$ and let $\map{f,g}{\N}{\leftclosed{0}{\infty}}$
  be such that
  for all natural numbers $F(n)=f(n)$ and $G(n)=g(n)$.
  If $\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)/G(x)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup{\set{\infty}}$
  then $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{example}
  For polynomial functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule works well.
  Consider again $\fcn{f,g}{\N}{\N}$ given by 
  $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=2n^2+3n+4$.
  On the associated real functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule gives this. 
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2+3x+4}{x^2+5x+6}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x+3}{2x+5}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4}{2}
    =2
  \end{equation*}
  By \cref{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns} the limit of the natural number functions
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ is also~$2$.
  With that, \cref{lem:LHBigO} says that the two
  have the same growth rate, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.

  Similarly, for $f(n)=2n^2+3n+4$ and $g(n)=n^3+5n+6$,
  L'H\^opital's Rule shows that
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^3+5x+6}{2x^2+3x+4}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+5x+6}{4x+3}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{6x}{4}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  and so $f$ grows at a rate strictly slower than~$g$, that is,
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
  Non-polynomial functions also come up often.
  Compare $f(n)=n^2$ with $g(x)=2^n$,
  convert to real functions and apply L'H\^{o}pital's Rule.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x}{x^2}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot\ln(2)}{2x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot(\ln(2))^2}{2}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  Thus, by \cref{lem:LHBigO},
  $f$'s order is strictly less than $g$'s\Dash $f$ is in~$\bigOh(g)$ but
  $g$ is not in~$\bigOh(f)$.

  The same approach also shows that the order of $f(x)=\lg(x)$ is 
  strictly less than the order of $g(x)=x$.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\lg(x)}{x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)\cdot(1/\ln(2))}{1}
    =0
  \end{equation*}
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
  The order $\bigOh(n\cdot \lg(n))$ appears often.
  We will show that it equals $\bigOh(n!)$ by mutual containment of sets.
  L`H\^{o}pital's Rule is not a good fit here because of the factorial so
  we appeal directly to the definition of big-O.
  
  Recall that logarithmic functions turn multiplication into addition:
  $\lg(a\cdot b)=\lg(a)+\lg(b)$.
  The $\bigOh(\lg(n!))\subseteq\bigOh(n\lg(n))$ direction follows from
  this.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots \lg(2) + \lg(1)            \\
            &< \lg(n) + \lg(n)   + \cdots + \lg(n)+\lg(n) = n\cdot\lg(n)
  \end{align*}
  The $\bigOh(n\lg(n))\subseteq\bigOh(\lg(n!))$ direction is
  a bit trickier.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(2) + \lg(1)   \\
            &> \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2})   \\
            &> \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2}) \\
            &=(n/2)\cdot\lg(n/2)                      \\
            &=(n/2)\bigl[ \lg(n)-\lg(2) \bigr]
             =(1/2)\cdot n\lg(n)-(1/2)\cdot n\lg(2)
  \end{align*}
\end{example}

Programming~101. 
It is convenient because it gives a rough metric to compare 
algorithms.

We use the boundary between polynomial growth and exponential growth
as a division between runtime behavior that is conceivably useful in practice
and behavior that is not useful.
A problem is \definend{untractable} if the best algorithm to solve it
is superpolynomial and it is \definend{tractable} if the best algorithm
is subexponential.
(In practice, algorithms with a polynomial growth of a high degree, such as
$\bigOh(n^{10})$ don't come up very much.
No one knows why.)

\section{\textbf{NP}}
These two  
computational problems seem similar.

\begin{example}
Formally, a mathematical proof as a sequence of statements,
$\sigma_0$, $\sigma_1$, \ldots, $\sigma_k$,
where the final statement is the conclusion. 
Each statement either is itself an axiom or
else follows from the statements before it
by an application
of one of the agreed-upon derivation rules.
From experience we know that finding the proof of a desired  
conclusion can be very hard; often it seems to take a leap of insight.
Trying to brute-force it by taking all combinations of 
all the axioms and all the derivation rules would be very slow.
In contrast, if we were given the 
sequence of statements along with a description
of each step's rules, and asked only to check the 
sequence for correctness,
then that would be easy and quick.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider a complicated propositional logic expression, say
$(\neg(P_1\vee (P_2\wedge P_3)\rightarrow \neg P_4)\leftrightarrow (P_2\rightarrow P_3)\rightarrow (P_1\wedge \neg P_4)$.
We want to find whether it is \definend{satisfiable}, 
whether there is any assignment of 
truth values $\true$ or~$\false$ to the 
variables $P_1,\idots, P_4$ that makes the statement
as a whole have the value $\true$.
We can imagine trying to answer by reasoning
in some way as, 
``Well, the formula has an if and only if arrow so I can see that
it boils down to \ldots''
but an algorithm that unfailingly finds the solution that way is not evident.
Alternatively, 
you could go through the possible assignments
$\seq{\false, \false, \false, \false}$, 
then $\seq{\false, \false, \false, \true}$, etc., 
until one causes the statement to yield $\true$, if one ever does.
However, if instead you were given an answer, an assignment to the variables,
and only asked to verify that it solves the problem, then you 
could do that quickly.
\end{example}

These seem alike in that, putting aside brute force,
we feel that for each finding an answer is hard and somehow involves creativity.
But if we are simply handed a solution then verifying that it is right 
calls only for a dull calculation.

That is, there are problems where finding an answer is quick and easy.
The two problems above seem different\Dash they \emph{feel} hard to solve, 
although
they have the property that once an answer is given then it is easy
to check.
Despite this very strong feeling, though, we are today unable
to prove that they are of different hardnesses.
This question of whether we can tell the two apart
is certainly the sexiest one in Computing today.
One reason it is attractive is that many pressing problems
have been shown to be like the proof-generating or satisfiability questions:
they don't appear to be solvable efficiently
their solutions can be verified quickly.
however, we cannot today prove that  
that is the case.  




\section{Complexity classes}
 
\begin{definition}[the complexity class $\compclass{NP}$]
A language $\lang{L}$ is a member of 
$\compclass{NP}$ if there is a polynomial $\map{p}{\N}{\N}$ and a
polynomial time Turing machine $\TM$, the \definend{verifier}
for $\lang{L}$, such that 
\begin{equation*}
  \forall \sigma\in\strings 
  \big[ \sigma\in\lang{L} 
     \,\Longleftrightarrow\, 
     \exists w\in \set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}(\TM(\seq{\sigma,w})=1)  \big]
\end{equation*}
($w$ is the \definend{witness} or \definend{certificate} for $\sigma$).
\end{definition}

Note that the witness is restricted in length so that the verifier,
which as a Turing machine reads one bit at a time, can 
read it through in polynomial time.
Note also that we can take the polynomial~$p$ to be trivial $p(i)=0$
to get that $\compclass{P}\subseteq\compclass{NP}$.

\end{exercise}
\end{exercises}
