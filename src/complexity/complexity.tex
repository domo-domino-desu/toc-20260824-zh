In the first part of this book we asked what can be done mechanically.
Once you know that a task can be done at all then a natural next step is to
do it efficiently. in a practical time
(or space, or some other computational resource).

The Theory of Computing began in the 1930's.
There were no physical computers and 
the theory's initial phase had an abstract mathematical feel.
Researchers including Turing, Church,
and Kleene were driven by considerations such as the
\textit{Entscheidungsproblem}.
Their subject was interesting, the questions compelling, and there were
plenty of problems.

When actual computers became increasingly available, people in many fields
began working on algorithms to solve their problems, 
Theory of Computing researchers responded.
Starting in the 1970's and gaining strength during the 1980's and 1990's,
the subject has become driven more by
questions at least originating in applied fields.
These fields need that the answers are
feasible, so
concerns such as algorithm running time have grown in relative importance.

Because of this,
we start this part by reviewing the orders of growth of functions.
As this is a review, we will not give a detailed development,
just enough for our needs.

\section{Big O}
% For a problem there may be many algorithms.
% We typically want one that uses little time,
% or little of some other resource.
An algorithm uses resources 
at a rate that depends on the size of its input.
We have tools to describe rates.

We will review the basics of the growth rate of functions.
We will not give a full description, just enough for our needs,
and we will leave the proofs as exercises.

First, an anecdote.
Here is a multiplication problem.
% How long does it take?
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{array}{@{}r@{}}
    678\\
    \times 42\\
   \hline
    1356\\
   +27120\\  \hline
   28476
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
In grade school we learn the algorithm that for
each digit of the multiplier~$42$, we 
multiply each digit of the multiplicand~$678$.
That's a nested loop.
So the natural hypothesis is that multiplication of two $n$-bit numbers
requires, at worst, a time of about $n^2$ ticks.

% https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Andrej_Nikolajewitsch_Kolmogorov.jpg
\margingraphic{0.20\textwidth}{complexity/pix/Kolmogorov.jpg}{\label{fig:kolmogorov}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov}{Andrey Kolmogorov 1903--1987}}
In 1960 A~Kolmogorov organized a seminar at Moscow State University aimed at
proving this.
Before the next meeting one of his students,
\citetext{A~Karatsuba}{See \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Karatsuba}.},
had found that it is false.
Karatsuba produced a 
\citetext{clever algorithm}{%
  The idea is to let $k = \ceiling{n/2}$ 
  and write $x = x_1 2^k + x_0$ and $y = y_1 2^k + y_0$
  (so $678=21 \cdot 2^5+6$ and $42=1\cdot 2^5+10$). 
  Then we have $xy = A\cdot 2^{2k} + B\cdot 2^k + C$ 
  where $A = x_1 y_1$, and $B = x_1 y_0 + x_0 y_1$, and~$C = x_0 y_0$
  (here, $28\,476=21\cdot 2^{10}+216\cdot 2^5+60$). 
  The multiplications by $2^{2k}$ and $2^{k}$ are just bit-shifts to known 
  locations independent of the values of $x$ and $y$, 
  so they don't affect the time much. 
  But the two multiplications for 
  $B$ seem remove all the advantage and still give $\Theta(n^2)$ time.
  However, what Karatsuba noted instead was that
  $B = (x_0 + x_1)\cdot (y_0 + y_1) - A - C$
  Boom: done.
  Just one multiplication.}
that used about
$n^{\lg(3)}\approx n^{1.585}$ ticks.
At the next meeting 
Kolmogorov explained the result 
and closed the seminar.

This story illustrates a theme in the Theory of Computation:
% perusing the Theory of Computation blog feed \autocite{blog:TOC}
% shows that
every day researchers
produce results saying, ``here is a job and here is a way to do it in less time,
or less space, etc.'' 
We are good at finding clever ways to solve a problem and thereby
reducing the upper bound
rate at which we consume some computation resource, such as time.
But we are not as good at proving nontrivial lower bounds, at saying
``no algorithm can do this job in less than this number of ticks.'' 
That's one reason why in the development below we will
mostly compare the growth rates of functions using a measure that is
about `less than or equal to'.

\subsection{Definition}
We are studying functions that describe machine resource use.
Typically the size of the machine's inputs and of its outputs,
are natural numbers.

\begin{definition}
A \definend{natural number function} has domain~$\N$ and
codomain~$\N$.\footnotemark[1]
\end{definition}\footnotetext[1]{
  In this discussion we have already used a function, $\lg$,
  that takes real number inputs and gives real number outputs,
  gives negative outputs for some of those inputs,
  and is not even defined on the input~$0$.
  But all of the functions that
  we will use are defined and nonnegative eventually,
  meaning that there is a large enough $N\in\N$
  so that if $x>N$ then $f(x)$ is defined and nonnegative.
  So that won't interfere with the use of the big-O definition.
  And, if we are working with a function whose
  outputs are not restricted to the natural numbers then
  we can instead consider the ceiling $\ceiling{f}$.
  So for our purposes natural number functions suffice.}

\begin{definition}  \label{def:BigO}
  Let $g$ be a natural number function.
  Then \definend{$\bigOh(g)$}, read ``big-O of $g$,''
  is the collection of natural number functions~$f$
  satisfying:~there are numbers $N,C\in\N$ so that 
  whenever $n\geq N$ then $C\cdot g(n)\geq f(n)$. 
  We say that \definend{$f$ is in $\bigOh(g)$},
  or that \definend{$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$},
  or that \definend{$f=\bigOh(g)$}.
\end{definition}

Think of `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' as meaning that
$f$'s growth rate is less than or equal to $g$'s rate
(some authors highlight this intuition by writing $f\ll g$
if $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$ but it is not the case that
$g$ is $\bigOh(f)$).
These illustrate.
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo10.pdf}
  \hspace{3em}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo11.pdf}  
\end{center}
The sketch on the left has $g$ accelerating away
from~$f$ so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ since
its rate is smaller.
The sketch on the right is that $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ because
the rates are alike.

\begin{remark}
  \begin{parenum}
    \item
      We use the letter `O' because this is about the order of growth.
      % although the $\bigOh$~symbol only expresses an
      % upper bound.
    \item
      The `$f=\bigOh(g)$' notation is awkward but 
      standard.\footnotemark[2]
      The notation `$f\in\bigOh(g)$' is correct but not common.
    \item
      Some authors allow negative real outputs and write the
      inequality with absolute values, 
      $f(x)\leq C\cdot |g(x)|$.
    \item
      You often see constructions such as `$n^2+5n+6$ is $\bigOh(n^2)$',
      replacing the named functions $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=n^2$
      with expressions.
    \item
      Sometimes you see `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' stated as
      `$f(n)$ is $\bigOh(g(n))$'.
      Strictly speaking, this is
      wrong\Dash $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ are numbers, not functions, and big-O
      is a relationship between functions.
  \end{parenum}
  \end{remark}\footnotetext[2]{%
        One way that it is awkward is that it does not follow the usual rules
        of equality so for instance
        you cannot rewrite it as `$\protect\bigOh(g)=f$'.
        Another way is that $n=\bigOh(n^2)$ and $n^2=\bigOh(n^2)$ does not
        allow us to conclude that $n=n^2$.}

\begin{example} \label{ex:BigOFromDefn}
  To directly apply the definition, given two functions we must produce
  suitable $N$ and~$C$, and verify that they work.
  Let $f(n)=n^2$ and $g(n)=n^3$.
  Then $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$, as $N=2$ and $C=1$ witness.
  The verification is that $n>2$ implies that $g(n)=n^3=n\cdot n^2$ is greater
  than $2\cdot n^2$, which is greater than $n^2=1\cdot f(n)$.

  Similarly, if $f(n)=5n^2$ and $g(n)=n^4$ then to show $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$
  take $N=2$ and $C=2$.
  The verification:
  $n> 2$ implies that $C\cdot n^4=2\cdot n^2\cdot n^2\geq 8n^2> 5\cdot n^2$.
\end{example}

We've given the intuition that $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ if
$f$'s growth rate is less than or equal to $g$'s growth rate.
That differs from saying that $f$'s values are eventually less
than~$g$'s.

\begin{example}
  Take $f(n)=n^2+1$ and~$g(n)=n^2$, so that $g(n)<f(n)$ for all~$n$.
  To verify that $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$, take $N=2$ and~$C=2$ and 
  if $n>N$ we can write $n=j+2$ for $j\in\N^+$, giving
  that $C\cdot g(n)=2n^2=2(j+2)^2=2j^2+8j+8$ is greater than
  $f(n)=(j+2)^2+1=j^2+4j+5$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The zero function~$Z$ is $\bigOh(g)$ for every
  $\map{g}{\N}{\N}$.
  Just take $N=0$ and~$C=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Some pairs of functions aren't comparable.
Let $g(n)=n^3$.
The function
\begin{equation*}
  f(n)=\begin{cases}
         n^2  &\case{if $n$ is even}  \\
         n^4  &\case{if $n$ is odd}
       \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
is not~$\bigOh(g)$ because there are
arbitrarily large inputs~$n$ where $C=2$ does not suffice:
if $n$ is odd and $n>2$  then $f(n)=n^4=n\cdot n^3> 2\cdot n^3$, and
similar reasoning works for any~$C$.
Likewise, $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$ because of its behavior even inputs.
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigOhReflexiveAndTransitive}
  The big-O relation is reflexive:~$f$ is $\bigOh(f)$.
  It is also transitive:~if $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and $g$ is~$\bigOh(h)$ then
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(h)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}
  Two natural number functions have
  \definend{the same growth rate} if both $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and also
  $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$.
  We say \definend{$f$ is $\bigTheta(g)$} or, what is the same thing,
  $g$ is~$\bigTheta(f)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigThetaEquivalence}
  The relation between functions of having the same growth rate
  is an equivalence.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic properties]  \label{lem:BigOAlgProps}
\begin{parenum}
\item
  If $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$ then 
  for any positive constant $a\in\R^+$, the function $af$ is $\bigOh(g)$.
\item
  Suppose that $f_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$ and $f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_1)$.
  Then the sum $f_0+f_1$ is $\bigOh(g)$, where
  $g(n)=\max(g_0(n),g_1(n))$.  
  In particular, if there is a function $\hat{g}$ where
  if both $f_0$ and $f_1$ are $\bigOh(\hat{g})$
  then $f_0+f_1$ is also $\bigOh(\hat{g})$.
  As well, 
  the product $f_0f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_0g_1)$.
\end{parenum}
\end{lemma}


\begin{example}
  The $\bigOh(f)$'s are sets.
  To illustrate, we will show that $\bigOh(n\cdot \lg(n))$
  equals $\bigOh(\lg(n!))$ by mutual containment.
  
  Recall that logarithmic functions turn multiplication into addition:
  $\lg(a\cdot b)=\lg(a)+\lg(b)$.
  The $\bigOh(\lg(n!))\subseteq\bigOh(n\lg(n))$ direction follows from
  this.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots \lg(2) + \lg(1)            \\
            &< \lg(n) + \lg(n)   + \cdots + \lg(n)+\lg(n) = n\cdot\lg(n)
  \end{align*}
  The $\bigOh(n\lg(n))\subseteq\bigOh(\lg(n!))$ direction is
  trickier.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(2) + \lg(1)   \\
            &> \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2})   \\
            &> \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2}) \\
            &=(n/2)\cdot\lg(n/2)                      \\
            &=(n/2)\bigl[ \lg(n)-\lg(2) \bigr]
             =(1/2)\cdot n\lg(n)-(1/2)\cdot n\lg(2)
  \end{align*}
\end{example}


Directly applying the defintion of
$\bigOh$, as in \cref{ex:BigOFromDefn}, is sometimes the best way
to go.
But often
this result makes the job easier.

\begin{theorem}  \label{lem:LHBigO}
  Let $f,g$ be natural number functions and suppose that 
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup\set{\infty}$.
  \begin{parenum}
  \item If $L=\infty$ then $f$ grows faster than~$g$,
    meaning that $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$ but $f$ is not $\bigOh(g)$.
  \item If $L=0$ then $g$ grows faster than~$f$,
    so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
  \item If $L$ is strictly between $0$ and~$\infty$
  then the two functions have the same growth rate so that
    $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$ and~$g$ is $\bigOh(f)$,
    that is, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.
  \end{parenum}
\end{theorem}

To apply that theorem, we use 
this result from first semester calculus.

\begin{theorem}[L'H\^{o}pital's Rule]
Suppose that $\map{f,g}{\R}{\R}$ are such that both
$f(x)\to\infty$ and $g(x)\to\infty$ as $x\to\infty$.
If 
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f^\prime(x)/g^\prime(x)$ exists and equals
$L\in\R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$
then
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{theorem}

Taking the derivative requires functions with inputs that are real numbers,
not natural numbers.
So we need the next observation.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns}
  Let $\map{F,G}{\R}{\R}$ and let $\map{f,g}{\N}{\leftclosed{0}{\infty}}$
  be such that
  for all natural numbers $F(n)=f(n)$ and $G(n)=g(n)$.
  If $\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)/G(x)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup{\set{\infty}}$
  then $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{example}
  For polynomial functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule works well.
  Consider again $\fcn{f,g}{\N}{\N}$ given by 
  $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=2n^2+3n+4$.
  On the associated real functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule gives this. 
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2+3x+4}{x^2+5x+6}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x+3}{2x+5}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4}{2}
    =2
  \end{equation*}
  Then \cref{lem:LHBigO} (along with \cref{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns})
  says that the two
  have the same growth rate, that is, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.

  Similarly, for $f(n)=2n^2+3n+4$ and $g(n)=n^3+5n+6$,
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^3+5x+6}{2x^2+3x+4}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+5x+6}{4x+3}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{6x}{4}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  shows that $f$ grows at a rate strictly less than~$g$'s rate, that is,
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example} \label{ex:CompareLogToPower}
For any power~$d>0$ the polynomial function~$x^d$
grows more quickly than the logarithmic function:
$\log_b(x)$ is~$\bigOh(x^d)$.
Verify it with L'Hopital's Rule.
(After \cref{ex:BitsToRepresentANumber} we noted that
no matter what the base, all logarithmic functions have the
same order of growth.
Here we use $\ln$, with base~$e$, because its derivative is easy.)
\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln(x)}{x^d}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)}{x^d}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x^{d+1}}
  =0
\end{equation*}
Examples are that $\lg(x)$ is $\bigOh(x)$,
that $\lg(x)$ is $\bigOh(x^{0.1})$,
and that $\lg(x)$ is $\bigOh(x^{0.01})$.
None of these comparisons reverse; \cref{exer:LogSmallerThanPower} shows that
$x^d$ is not~$\bigOh(\log_b(x))$.

The log-linear function $x\cdot\lg(x)$ has a similar relationship to the
polynomials $x^d$, where $d>1$.
\begin{multline*}
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x\ln(x)}{x^d}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x\cdot (1/x)+1\cdot\ln(x)}{dx^{d-1}}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1+\ln(x)}{dx^{d-1}}          \\
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)}{d(d-1)x^{d-2}}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{d(d-1)x^{d-1}}
  =0
\end{multline*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  To compare the exponential $g(n)=2^n$ with the polynomial $f(n)=n^2$,
  convert to real functions and apply L'H\^{o}pital's Rule.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x}{x^2}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot\ln(2)}{2x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot(\ln(2))^2}{2}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  Thus,
  $f$'s order is strictly less than $g$'s\Dash $f$ is in~$\bigOh(g)$ but
  $g$ is not in~$\bigOh(f)$.
\end{example}

That example is a blueprint for the single most important fact about
the growth rate of functions.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:PolySlowerThanExp} 
Polynomial functions grow more slowly than exponential functions:~for
any base $C>1$, if $f$ is a polynomial function
then $f$ is~$\bigOh(C^n)$ but
$C^n$ is not $\bigOh(f)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{exer:PolySlowerThanExp}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Discussion}
We use Big~O to classify algorithms by how much they use 
computing resources.
The bottom line is that Big~O is about scalability\Dash 
something that is $\bigOh(n^2)$ scales worse than something that is 
$\bigOh(n \lg n)$, but better than something that is
$\bigOh(n^3)$.  % credit: https://softwareengineering.stackexchange.com/a/20833
Is it any more complicated than that?

Yes, there are some points that traditionally give folks trouble.
The first is that Big~O is for algorithms, not programs.
For example, compare these program snippets.
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.4\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
for(i=0; i<10; ++i){
  x=10;
  find_clique(G,x,i);
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\hspace{2em}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.4\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
x=10;
for(i=0; i<10; ++i){
  find_clique(G,x,i);
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
The left one makes the mistake of putting
\codeinline{x=10} inside the loop, which costs the time for nine assignments.
But Big~O disregards this constant difference in time.
That is, Big~O is not suited to describing programs because it
abstracts away details at that level.
Big~O's level is that of algorithms.

% Besides, a program is written for, and runs on, a specific computing platform.
% Each platform has its own startup costs, and other details.
% It is all too low-level for us here.
% We will work at the abstraction level of algorithms.

That brings up the second point.
Remember that an algorithm is tied to assumptions about
an underlying computing model.
To find the Big~O behavior we must specify the costs for that model.
As an example, here is the time consumption 
definition for the model with which we are most familiar, the Turing machine.

\begin{definition} \label{def:TMTime}
A Turing machine $\TM$ 
\definend{runs in time $\map{t_{\TM}}{\kleenestar{\Sigma}}{\N}$} 
if  that function gives the 
number of steps the machine takes to halt
on input~$\sigma\in\kleenestar{\Sigma}$.
If the machine does not halt then $t_{\TM}(\sigma)=\infty$.
The machine  
\definend{runs in input length time $\map{\hat{t}_{\TM}}{\N}{\N}$} 
if $\hat{t}_{\TM}(n)$ is the maximum of the $t(\sigma)$ over all 
inputs~$\sigma$ of length~$n$.
Finally, the machine 
\definend{runs in time that is $\bigOh(f)$} 
if $\hat{t}_{\TM}$ is~$\bigOh(f)$. 
\end{definition}

A model
that is widely used in Computational Complexity because
it is a fair fit for the computing
environment in which many algorithms will be implemented
is the
\definend{Integer Random Access Machine (RAM)}.
Whereas Turing machines store in each cell only a
single bit, this model stores arbitrarily-large integers.
And whereas to get to a stored value
a Turing machine may have to spend a lot of steps moving the I/O
head to that cell, this model is `random access' because it 
retrieves the contents of any memory address in one step.
This model also typically assumes a single step for
elementary operations such as addition or multiplication.

That is, the second point is that to use Big~O to select a best algorithm,
we must be aware that 
algorithms can change their 
Big-O characteristics when brought to different computing models.
For instance, if we start with an algorithm intended for an Integer RAM
and ty to run it on a Turing machine, 
the simulation may cost us as much as $n^3$ time, so that if the algorithm is 
$\bigOh(n^2)$ on the Integer RAM
then on the Turing machine it is $\bigOh(n^5)$.
So we settle on a model and then use Big~O to help
pick an algorithm associated with that model.

% Discussion started as: https://cs.stackexchange.com/questions/9957/justification-for-neglecting-constants-in-big-o
OK, so we've settled on a model.
Our third point is that
\citetext{the definitions of Big-O and Big-Theta ignore constants}{%
  This discussion originated as \autocite{SE:IgnoreConstants}.};
does that potentially make the definition useless
for judging the practicality of an algorithm?
Imagine
an algorithm that takes time given by the quadratic function $C\cdot n^2$.
An enormous coefficient such as $C=2^{100}$ would make it
just as impractical as one that takes exponential time.
% \footnote{%
%   Although, in practice, often when an algorithm is discovered that has a
%   very large leading coefficient then soon researchers develop
%   improvements that lower the coefficient to a usable level.
%   In the same way, while in the past we have sometimes seen
%   algorithms that are polynomial but with 
%   large powers, such as $n^{10}$, often these powers have fallen
%   as researchers refine the approach.}
Similarly, an enormous $N$ may destroy our ability to compare
the growth rates for input sizes that we will every see in
practical circumstances.
Why ignore the constants?

One answer is that in a real life situation, to a person coding on a
particular platform,
finding these constants is very hard.
They involve the computer's exact hardware details
such as the memory addressing and paging, cache hits, and whether
the CPU can do some operations in parallel.
And, our experience is that very little benefit
comes from knwing them\Dash we never seem to find that the constants are
so enormous that they change what we do by much.

\margingraphic[l]{0.20\textwidth}{complexity/pix/knuth.jpg}{\label{fig:Knuth}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Knuth}{Donald Knuth b~1938}}
Even if we abstract away the details of today's machines, the
difficulty is that we have no reliable standard unit.
Machines on which algorithms are implemented vary widely.
Even if we forget about such things as Turing machines and just focus on
the architecture of the machines
that we have on our desks and in our pockets they
still vary,
for instance in how long they take to do a division or to write to memory.
We could try to agree on a standard reference machine\Dash
for instance, this is the approach taken in  D~Knuth's
\textit{Art of Computer Programming} series where he does a great
deal of operation counting\Dash
but we would have to 
\citetext{update the standard periodically}{%
  Even Knuth had to update his standard, from the machine language 
  MIX to MMIX.}.
So published results from, say, a decade ago would be problematic
because they refer to an old standard.
Being reference independent is an advantage, and in a
quickly changing field, almost a necessity.

Being reference independent means ignoring constants
and instead making relative statements.
Here is an analogy:~absolute distance measurement
means specifying units such as miles or kilometers.
Being able to make statements irrespective of the units requires
making relative statements such as,
``from here, New York City is twice as far as Boston.'' 

Adopting definitions that ignore constants has the advantage
% that we can do an analysis by
% counting the number of elementary operations, 
we do not have to worry % about different costs of individual operations,
about the difference between the cost of a division and the
cost of a memory write,
as long as those costs lie between reasonable limits.
However, the price is that
constant factors are meaningless,
since changing units is inseparable from changing constant factors; just as
changing from miles to kilometers requires multiplying by the constant~$1.61$.

\margingraphic{0.34\textwidth}{complexity/pix/xkcdbigo.png}{\label{fig:xkcdBigO}\href{https://xkcd.com/342/}{Courtesy \url{xkcd.com}}}
In short,
Big~O notation ignores constants because it is
a unit free, relative measurement.
It is not absolute.; it is intended only to measure performance variation.
% The advantage is the gain in implementation independence
% by allowing analysis that ignores the relative costs of
% elementary operations, as long as these costs are bounded.
% But the consequence is that constant factors are meaningless.

Big~O expresses how an algorithm
performs as the input size grows.
Thus, we say that a function that takes $3n$ steps is $\bigOh(n)$
in order to express that, roughly,
doubling the input size will no more than double the number of steps taken.
Similarly, if an algorithm is $\bigOh(n^2)$ then
doubling the input size will at most
quadruple the number of steps, and
$\bigOh(\lg n)$ means that doubling the input size will increase the number
of steps by at most some constant.

We can use Big~O to compare how algorithms
perform as the input size grows.
But it is only a rough comparison;
it cannot say with great precision when two algorithms are put into code,
which will be absolutely faster.\footnote{%
  Use benchmarks.}

% https://stackoverflow.com/a/19647659
That leads us to our fourth point,
\citetext{another concern involving the definition of Big~O and Big~Theta}{%
  This discussion originated as \autocite{SE:pseudopolynomial}.}.
Understanding how an algorithm
performs as the input size grows
requires a definition of input size.
There are two natural definitions and we
must choose the one that best fits what we are modelling. 

Consider a naive algorithm for factoring numbers that, given input $n$,
tests each $k\in\set{2,\ldots\,n-1}$
to see if it divides~$n$. %\footnote{%
%   This is not the best algorithm, but a good illustration.}
If~$n$ is prime then it tests all those numbers, which is
a worst-case of roughly~$n$-many divisions.
We can take
the size of an input~$n$ to be the number of bits needed
to represent~$n$ in binary,
approximately the base~$2$ logarithm, $\lg n$.
So for this algorithm the input is of size about~$\lg n$
and the number of operations is about~$n$.
That makes the number of operations exponential in the input
size\Dash in this model this algorithm is 
$\bigOh(2^b)$, where $b$ is the number of input bits.

In contrast, most people's experience from programming class is that this
algorithm would be described as linear, as $\bigOh(n)$, because
for the number~$n$ there are about $n$-many divisions.
How to explaiun the difference?

This is another example of the effect of each algorithm's subtle reliance
on a computing model.
In many application situations the programmer makes
the engineering judgement that
input will fit into a fixed-sized computer word, such as a $64$~bit word.
Imagine we are studying the naive factoring algorithm.
We judge that for every use of our program the input will
fit into a word.
This means that we are selecting a computation model, such as the
integer RAM model,
where larger numbers take the same time to input as smaller ones.
Then the prior paragraph applies, so the algorithm associated with
this model is linear.

For another example, if we are studying selection sort and after
careful thought we decide on the specification that
for every use of our program the input will
fit into a word.
Under this model, with $n$ integer elements of input
the number of bits is $64n$.
Analysis of selection sort gives that the time it takes grows quadratically
so in bits we get $\bigOh((64n)^2)$.
That's $\bigOh(n^2)$.

% Another example would be in programming a depth-first tree traversal.
% You decide to represent nodes as successive integers that
% fit into a single 64 bit word.
% This may well be a sound application engineering decision since
% then you can store awfully big trees, 
% that will take a long time to process,
% and so this engineering judgement may be right for your application.
% In this case the fixed-size input storage
% decision changes the analysis of the relationship between
% input size and output size, since now the input size is measured as
% a function of the number of nodes~$n$.

So this is an applications versus theory thing: in the common programming
situation where the bit size of the inputs is bounded,
the runtime behavior is conveniently described as a function of
the number of inputs~$n$.
In the theoretical model, accepting integers that are arbitrarily large, it is 
the bit size of those inputs.
An algorithm whose behavior as a function of the input is polynomial,
but whose behavior as a function of the bit size of the input is
exponential, is said to be \definend{pseudopolynomial}.

A final point about describing algorithm behavior with Big~O.
When we are analyzing an algorithm we can look for a number of
different behavior descriptions.
For instance, we can describe the time the algorithm takes, or the space.
But we can also analyze the worst case behavior, or the average behavior.
For example, the widely used sorting algorithm quicksort
takes quadratic time $\bigOh(n^2)$ at worst.
But on average it is fast, $\bigOh(n\lg n)$.
So, algorithm analysis can happen on a number of fronts.

  Use L'Hopitals' Rule to show that $(\ln(x))^2$ is $\bigOh(x)$.
  Show also that $(\ln(x))^2$ is $\bigOh(x^p)$ for any power~$p\geq 1$.
  \begin{answer}
  We have

    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(\ln(x))^2}{x}

\section{Drivers}
Much of the present work in the Theory of Computation is driven by problems.
Often these come from a field outside the subject and are taken
up by computation researchers who
try to classify problems by their inherent computational difficulty.
`Inherent' means that from among 
all the algorithms for a problem,  we
are interested in one that uses the fewest resources.

% This section outlines how we approach problems in this Part, and then introduces
% a collection of ilustrative problems.






\subsection{Strings, problems, and classes}
To suit this Part's shift in focus
we will make some adjustments to the approach that we have taken so far.

The first adjustment is a mindset, a thesis.
We will take the point of view that 
\citetext{everything is bitstrings}{This discussion is based on R J Lipton's wonderful blog \protect\url{https://rjlipton.wordpress.com/2010/11/07/what-is-a-complexity-class/
}.}.
Whereas earlier we might say,
``use the Cantor function to encode that as a number,''
from now on we will informally take
``Let $n$ be an natural number \ldots''
to mean 
``Consider the binary representation of the number~$n$.''
Similarly,
``Let $\mathcal{G}$ be a graph \ldots'' translates to
``Assume that we have a reasonably efficient way to encode the graph as
a string of \str{0}'s and \str{1}'s.''
We are used to representing mathematical objects
such as numbers, matrices, and graphs in this way.
But this mindset is more:~we have come to think 
that bitstrings can represent anything
that we study.
This includes pictures
from selfies to the Old Masters,
music such as 
\citetext{Beethoven's 9th Symphony}{The official story is that
the $72$ minute length of a CD was chosen to hold this piece.}, or
a movie that makes things that have never happened
seem real.

In some ways this is \citetext{like Church's Thesis}{%
  We may call it Lipton's Thesis, after
  \url{https://rjlipton.wordpress.com/2010/11/07/what-is-a-complexity-class/}.}.
We cannot prove that it holds, but both reflection and experience
strongly suggest that it is so.

The second adjustment is that
while we will continue to talk informally about problems,
formally from this point forward we take `problem'
to mean a decision problem for a language.
Consider the function problem that is given $n\in\N$
and finds its prime factors.
Informallly we still think of it in the same way, but in formally
stating results, from here on 
we will take it to be 
the problem of deciding membership in
$\set{\sigma=\str{1}^n0\str{1}^{p_0}\cdots 0\str{1}^{p_{k-1}}\in\kleenestar{\B}\suchthat\text{$p_0$, \ldots{} $p_{k-1}$ are the prime factors of $n$}}$.
% Thus, we take the problem of deciding whether a
% number is composite to be, formally,
% the problem of computing membership in the language
% $\set{\sigma\in\kleenestar{\B}\suchthat \text{$\sigma$ represents a prime number in binary}}$.
(We will be sloppy about the distinction between the
decision problem for a language and the language itself; for instance, we
often use the letter $\prob$ for a problem.)

Is limiting ourselves to language decision problems too restricitve?
Consider the problem of finding integer roots for a
polynomial.
The function problem that starts with a polynomial and returns a
root, if any, intuitively seems slower than the problem of verifying
that a pair $\sequence{p,r}$ is in the language
$\set{\sequence{p,r}\suchthat p(r)=0}$.
However, we will consider classes
of problems that are closed under the difference, in that if  
a fast way to solve the decision problem is in the class then
we can translate it to 
a fast way to solve the function problem, and vice versa.

\begin{definition}
A \definend{problem} is a language decision problem.
\end{definition}

Because we are isolating language decision problems,
we will also adjust our Turing machines.
They now come with a subset of accepting states
and we define that such a machine solves a problem
if it halts on all inputs~$\sigma\in\kleenestar{\B}$,
and when it does halt it is in an accepting state 
exactly if $\sigma$ is in
the language.
% (This does not violate our understanding from the Halting Problem because
% if there is no such machine then just by definition,
% the problem isn't solved.)

Finally, the third adjustment is that we often want to consider a group of
problems.
An example is that we may want to study the collection of problems that are
tractable, for which there is a polynomial algorithm.
There are
\citetext{a great many ways to group problems together}{%
  At this writing there are 535 ways but the number changes frequently;
  see \protect\url{https://complexityzoo.uwaterloo.ca/Complexity_Zoo}.}.
This included grouping them by the use of computational resources other than
time, including space.
The most satisfactory definition for a grouping proves to be the one that
leaves the most freedom to consider whatever classes prove to be
of interest.

\begin{definition}
A \definend{complexity class} is a collection of problems.
\end{definition}

W~R Hamilton was a polymath whose genius was recognized early and he was 
given a sinecure as Astronomer Royal of Ireland.  
He made important contributions to classical mechanics, where his 
reformulation of Newtonian mechanics is now called Hamiltonian mechanics. 
Other work of his in physics helped develop classical field theories such as

In mathematics, he is best known as the
\citetext{inventor of the quaternion number system}{See \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_quaternions}.}.

\begin{problem}[Hamiltonian Circuit]
Given a graph, decide if it contains a \definend{Hamiltonian circuit},
a cyclic path that includes each vertex.
\end{problem}

As stated, this is a language decision problem that inputs a graph
and decides `yes' or `no', whether that graph has a circuit.
Another decision problem would be to input a pair and decide
if it is a member of
$\set{\sequence{\mathcal{G},C}\suchthat\text{$C$ is a circuit of $\mathcal{G}$}}$.
Which version of the problem we study depends on exactly which question
we want to answer, but as stated above, formally we are taking
the problems to be language decision problems.

Of the other problems described in this section, some are stated as
decision problems, but some are given as function problems or optimization
problems.
For these, there are exercises asking you to convert them.

A special case of the Hamiltonian Circuit problem
is the \definend{Knight's Tour},
to use a chess knight to make a circuit of the squares on the board.

In graph terms, there are sixty four vertices,

An edge goes between two vertices if they are connected by a single knight move.
(Recall that a knight moves three squares at a time,
two in one direction and then one perpendicular to that direction.)
The knight's tour is a Hamiltonian circuit problem on that graph.

The Hamiltonian Circuit problem has another famous variant.

\begin{problem}[Travelling Salesman]  \label{ex:TravellingSalesman}
Given a set $S=\set{c_0,\ldots c_{k-1}}$ whose elements are called
cities, and an array of distances $d(c_i,c_j)\in\N^+$ for all
$c_i\neq c_j$ (subject to $d(c_i,c_2)=d(c_j,c_i)$), 
find the cheapest circuit that visits every city.
\end{problem}

An instance starts with a map of the  
forty eight contiguous US~states, including the state capitals
along with the distances between them: Montpelier~VT to	Albany~NY is $203.08$
kilometers, etc.
From among all trips that visit each city and return back to the start,
such as 
$\text{Montpelier}\rightarrow\text{Albany}\rightarrow\text{Harrisburg}\rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow\text{Montpelier}$, we want the shortest one.

As stated, this is an optimization problem.
Formally, we will recast it as a
decision problem.
To do that, 
introduce a threshold bound $B\in\N$ and change the problem statement to
`decide if there is a circuit of length less than $B$'.
With it, to find the length of the shortest circuit we can first decide if
there is a trip bounded in length by $B=1$, if there is a 
trip bounded in length by $B=2$, etc.
If we have an algorithm for the decision problem, then we solve the
optimization problem by iterating until
we find such a bound.

Given a graph, find a circuit that traverses each edge once

\end{problem}

The next problem was discovered in 1852 by a young mathematician, F~Guthrie,
who was coloring a map of the counties of England.
He wanted adjacent counties to be colored differently and
he naturally wanted to use as few colors as possible.
His map required only four colors and he
conjectured that for any map at all, four colors suffice.

Guthrie imposed the condition that the map's regions must be contiguous,
and he defined `adjacent' to mean sharing a border that is an interval,
not just a point; see \cref{exer:FiveColors}.
On the right below is a graph version of the problem.
Counties are represented as vertices and edges connect them if they
are adjacent.
Because this graph arises from a map, it is \definend{planar}\Dash
we can draw it in the plane so that its edges do not cross.
So, the Four Color problem is to, given a planar graph,
separate the vertices into no more than four categories, the colors,
so that adjacent vertices are in different categories.

    The map at the time had some counties that were non-contiguous.}}\centering

Guthrie consulted his former professor, A~De~Morgan,
who was also unable to either prove or disprove the conjecture.
But he did make the problem famous by promoting it among his
mathematical friends, including Hamilton.
It remained unsolved until 1976 when
K~Appel and W~Haken announced 
that they reduced the proof to 1\,936 cases, and gotten a
computer to check those cases.
This was the first major proof that was done on a computer and it was
controversial, although now that is routine.

\begin{problem}[Graph Colorability] \label{prob:GraphColorability}
Given a graph and a bound $B\in\N$, decide whether the 
graph is \definend{$B$-colorable}, 
whether we can partition its vertices into $B$-many classes 
$\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{C}_0\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{C}_{B-1}$ such
that never are two same-class vertices connected by an edge.
\end{problem}


\margingraphic{0.2\textwidth}{complexity/pix/boole.jpg}{\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Boole}{George Boole 1815--1864}} % See page for author [Public domain], via Wikimedia Commons; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AGeorge_Boole_color.jpg
We close with a problem that seems easy to do by hand
but that gets harder rapidly as the size of the problem grows.
In 1847 by G~Boole outlined the subject that we today call Boolean algebra in
\textit{An Investigation of the Laws of Thought on Which are Founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities}.
It provided a systemmatic mathematical formalism
for simple logical relations.

Recall that a variable is \definend{Boolean} if it takes on a value of
$T$ or~$F$.
A \definend{Boolean function} inputs and outputs tuples of those values. 
We can make \definend{Boolean formulas} by connecting variables using the
binary \definend{and operator~$\wedge$},
the binary \definend{or operator~$\vee$},
and the unary \definend{not operator~$\neg$}.
Here is a formula with three variables.
\begin{equation*}
  f(P,Q,R)=(P\vee Q)\wedge (P\vee \neg Q)\wedge (\neg P\vee Q)
  \wedge (\neg P\vee \neg Q\vee \neg R) % \wedge (\neg P\vee R)
\end{equation*}
(Formulas with clauses of $\vee$'s connected with $\wedge$'
are in \definend{disjunctive normal form}.)   
A Boolean formula is \definend{satisfiable} the function that it
defines if there is some combination
of input $T$'s and $F$'s so that the formula evaluates to~$T$.

This \definend{truth table} shows the input-output behavior of the
function defined by that formula.
\begin{center} % \definecolor{rowcolor}{gray}{.93} \rowcolors{3}{rcolor}{}
\begin{tabular}{ccc|ccccc}
  $P$  &$Q$  &$R$  &$P\vee Q$ &$P\vee \neg Q$ &$\neg P\vee Q$ &$\neg P\vee \neg Q\vee \neg R$ &$f(P,Q,R)$ \\ \hline    
  $F$  &$F$  &$F$  &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $F$  &$F$  &$T$  &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $F$  &$T$  &$F$  &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $F$  &$T$  &$T$  &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\[1ex]     
  $T$  &$F$  &$F$  &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $T$  &$F$  &$T$  &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $T$  &$T$  &$F$  &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ \\     
  $T$  &$T$  &$T$  &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$F$      
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
That $T$ in the final column witnesses that this formula is satisfiable.

\begin{problem}[Satisfiability, $\SAT$]  \label{prob:SAT}
Given a propositional logic formula,
decide if it is satisfiable.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[$3$-Satisfiability, $3$-$\SAT$]
Given a propositional logic formula in conjunctive normal form,
in which each clause has at most three variables,
decide if it is satisfiable.
\end{problem}

We will have much more to say about this problem in this book's final
part, but until then observe that the number of rows in the truth table
is $2$ raised to the number
of input variables.
So solving this problem, at least at first glance, appears to take
exponential time.
How fast it can be done is a very important problem, as we will see in
later sections.

\begin{problem}[Minimum Spanning Tree] \label{prob:minspanningtree}
Given an edge-weighted undirected graph,

\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Divisors]
Given a number~$n\in\N$, factor it; that is, produce a number $1<a<n$
that is a divisor of~$n$.
\end{problem}

When the numbers are sufficiently large,
\citetext{we know of no efficient algorithm to find divisors}{% https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_factorization
  An effort in 2009 to factor a $768$-bit number ($232$-digits) used
  hundreds of machines and took two years.
  The researchers estimated that a $1024$-bit number would take about
  a thousand times as long.}.\footnote{%
  No efficient algorithm is known on a non-quantum computer.}
However, it must be said that at this time
we also have no proof that no efficient algorithm exists.\footnote{%
  There is no proof despite centuries of ingenious attacks on the problems
  by many of the brightest minds of the past, and of today.
  The presumed difficulty of this problem is at the heart of widely used
  algorithms in cryptography.}
Not all numbers of a given length are equally hard to factor.
The hardest numbers to factor, using the best currently known techniques,
are semiprimes, the product of two prime numbers.

\begin{problem}[Prime Factorization]
Given a number~$n\in\N$, produce its decomposition into a product of primes.
\end{problem}

\citetext{Factoring enormous numbers remains hard}{%
  Finding factors has for many years been thought hard.
  For instance, a number is called a Mersenne prime
  if it is a prime number of the form $2^n-1$.
  They are named after M~Mersenne, a French friar and important figure in
  the early sharing of scientific results,
  who studied them in the early 1600's.
  He observed that
  if $n$ is prime then $2^n-1$ may be prime, for instance with $n=3$,
  $n=7$, $n=31$, and $n=127$.
  He suspected that others of that form were also prime, in particular
  $n=67$.

  On \ymd{1903}{10}{31}{} F~N Cole,
  then Secretary of the American Mathematical Society,
  made a presentation at a math meeting.
  When introduced, he went to the chalkboard and in complete silence
  computed $2^{67}-1=147\,573\,952\,589\,676\,412\,927$.
  He then moved to the other side of the board, wrote
  $193\,707\,721$ times $761\,838\,257\,287$, and worked through the
  calculation, finally finding equality.
  When he was done Cole returned to his seat, having not uttered a word
  in the hour-long presentation.
  His audience gave him a standing ovation.

  Cole later said that finding the factors had been a significant effort,
  taking ``three years of Sundays.''},
that we know of.
What about if you just want to know whether a number is prime or composite,
and don't care about its factors?

\begin{problem}[Composite]
Given a number~$n\in\N$, determine if it has any nontrivial factors;
that is, decide if there are numbers $1<a,b<n$ such that $ab=n$.
\end{problem}

\margingraphic{0.25\textwidth}{complexity/pix/aks.jpg}{\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AKS_primality_test}{Nitin Saxena (b~1981), Neeraj Kayal (b~1979), Manindra Agrawal (b~1966)}} % http://www.horizons-2000.org/9.%20Active%20Projects/read/PRIMES%20is%20in%20P.htm; came from MAA focus?
For many years the consensus among experts was that the Composite
problem was probably quite hard.\footnote{%
  There are a number of probabilistic algorithms that are often
  used in practice that can test
  primality very quickly, with an extremely small chance of error.}
After all, for centuries many very smart people had worked on composites
and primes, and none of them had produced a fast test.
But in 2002 Agrawal, N~Kayal, and N~Saxena
proved that primality testing can be done in time polynomial 
in the number of digits of the number.
At this point refinments of their technique run in
$\bigOh(n^6)$.
This is known as the AKS primality test.\footnote{
  At the time they did most of the work, Kayal and Saxena were undergraduates.}

On thing this test does is dramatically illustrate the lesson
that although experts are expert and
their opinions have value, nonetheless they can be wrong.
People producing a result that gainsays established orthodoxy
has happened before and will no doubt happen again\Dash
one correct proof outweighs any number of opinions.

\section{$\P$}
Our first computational complexity class is the collection of
the problems that we take to be tractable, the ones for which there
is an algorithm that can at least conceivably be used. 

\begin{definition}
A problem is a member of the class \definend{$\P$} if
there is an algorithm for it that runs in
polynomial time on a deterministic Turing machine.
\end{definition}

Note that it is wrong to say that an algorithm is in $\P$.
Rather, the members of $\P$ are problems, essentially languages,
for which there exists a decision algorithm that is sufficiently fast.
This extends to any complexity class.
The members are problems.


% This shows the class~$\P$ as a subset of the collection of all languages.
\begin{fig}[]{The bean encloses all problems, 
all decision problems for some $\lang\subseteq\kleenestar{\B}$.
Shaded is the class~$\P$.
The shading is in layers because
$\P$ contains problems such that there is a solution algorithm  
that is $\bigOh(1)$,
and also problems for which there is an algorithm that is $\bigOh(n)$,
ones that are $\bigOh(n^2)$, etc.}
  \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp01.pdf}}
  \hspace{0.6em}
  \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp02.pdf}}
\end{fig}

One problem that is a member of~$\P$ is that of deciding the
language of perfect squares
$\set{n\in\N\suchthat \text{there is $k\in\N$ with $k^2=n$}}$
since given a natural number, no matter how large,
we can quickly decide if it is a square by using Newton's method.
Here are some others:
(i)~deciding the language of correct sums
$\set{\sequence{a,b,c}\in\N^3\suchthat a+b=c}$,
(ii)~deciding the language of sorted strings
$\set{\sigma\in\kleenestar{\str{a},\ldots \str{z}}\suchthat \text{$\sigma$ is in alphabetical order}}$,
(iii)~deciding the language of correct matrix multiplication
$\set{\sequence{A,B,C}\suchthat\text{the matrices are such that $AB=C$}}$,
(iv)~deciding the language of primes
$\set{\str{1}^k\suchthat\text{$k$ is prime}}$,
(v)~deciding the language of reachable nodes
$\set{\sequence{\mathcal{G},v,\hat{v}}\suchthat\text{graph $\mathcal{G}$ has a path from $v\hat{v}$}}$.  




\subsection{Effect of the model of computation}
A problem is in~$\P$ if it has an algorithm that is polytime.
But each algorithm is based on an underlying computing model and
different models perform at different levels.
In particular, experience with
the Turing machine model gives a person the sense that they are slow, simply
because they involve a lot of moving on the tape.
Turing was concerned with nailing the
concept of computability, not with making his model efficient. 

However, experience
with a wide range of alternative computational models
proposed over the years shows that while using
Turing machines to simulate these other, perhaps more practical,
models introduces a slowdown, all in all Turing machines do pretty well.
Close analysis shows that
Turing machines are typically slower than other natural models\footnote{%
  Our definition of a `natural' model is one that
  was not invented in order to be a counterexample to this.}
by a factor of $n^2$ or $n^3$,
meaning that if we have a problem for which there is a
$\bigOh(n)$ algorithm on
another model then when translated to a Turing machine
algorithm it may be $\bigOh(n^4)$.
% Another example of this is that a multi-tape universal Turing machine
% only suffers a logarithmic slowdown factor in simulating any Turing machine.
A variation of Church's thesis posits that any
reasonable model of computation\footnote{%
  One definition of `reasonable' is ``in principle physically realizable''
  \autocite{BernsteinVazirani}.
} can be simulated on a
probabilistic Turing machine\footnote{%
A Turing machine with a random oracle.}
in polytime.
This thesis controversial and does not enjoy anything like
the support of the original Church's Thesis.
But its existence does demonstrate
that there is no counterexample of a reasonable model that cannot be
simulated quickly.\footnote{%
  There is a possible exception on the horizon. 
  Under the Quantum Computing model
  there are several problems with polytime solutions,
  including integer factorization,
  for which no polytime solution is known in any natural
  non-quantum model.
  Whether quantum computers will ever be practical is not
  at this moment perfectly clear but scientists and engineers are making
  progress developing such devices.}




  

 



\subsection{Naturalness}
The class~$\P$ is very important.
We will note several additional reasons why it is natural to
study.

The first was described in the prior subsection.
Over the past century researchers have devised a wide range of
models of computation.
All of them compute the same set of functions as Turing machines\Dash
otherwise Church's Thesis would be invalidated\Dash but at
varying speeds.
But all these models are within polytime of each other.
That is, 
one thing that makes $P$ a natural class is that it is
invariant under the choice of
computing model:~if a problem is in $P$ based on any familiar model
including Turing machines, Integer RAM, and Scheme programs, then
it is in~$P$ for all of them.
The differences between models gets
lost in the general polynomial sloppiness.
(Standard practice defines~$\P$ via Turing machines.)

Another reason is that $P$ is a natural class of problems to study
is that it has a number of appealing closure properties.
For instance, let $\map{f}{\N}{\N}$ be a computable function and consider
the decision problem for the language
$\prob_f=\set{\sequence{\bar{n},\bar{f(n)}}\in\kleenestar{\B}\suchthat n\in\N}$,
where the bars means that we represent the numbers in binary.
Then we can consider~$f$ to be a problem.
With that, $\P$ is closed under function addition,
scalar multipliation by an integer,
subtraction, multiplication, and composition.

Some more examples are that $\P$ is closed under concatenation,
so if $\lang\in\P$ and $\hat{\lang}\in\P$ then the concatenation
language $\lang\hat{\lang}$ is also an element of~$\P$.
The same holds for Kleene star.

\section{$\NP$}
Recall that a machine is nondeterminstic if
from a present configuration and input
it may pass to a next configuration where the number of next states
is zero, or one, or more.
That is, the output of the transition function is a set.
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta(q_i,T_p)=\set{\sequence{q_{j_0},T_{n_0}},\sequence{q_{j_1},T_{n_1}}\,\ldots}
\end{equation*}
For Turing machines, nondeterminism gives a completely
capable device, in the sense of Church's Thesis, that is
also unboundedly-parallel\Dash
at any step in a computation this machine can fork any finite number of
children, who can then continue on as multiple processes and can
go on to fork children of their own.

Recall also that we have two models of how these devices operate.
In the first, the machine's computation history is a tree and it
simultaneously computes all branches.
This fits exactly with the prior paragraph's `unboundedly parallel'
and also fits with our experience on everyday computers where, for instance,
you start a new process by opening a new window, and the machine can
have lots of them open at a time. 

In the second model the machine guesses which next state to follow and
then deterministically computes that branch of the computation tree.
This model can at first feel odd since our programming experience does not
have machines guessing; 
an alternative working is that an oracle, a
demon, tells the machine the correct branchings 
through the comptuation tree.

With the first model we say an input string is accepted if one of the 
branchs on the tree is an accepting branch.
With the second model we say the input
is accepted if there is a sequences of guesses that the machine could
make, or if there exists a sequence of
branching hints that the demon could produce,
that leads to an accepting state.
With those definitions, the two models are logically equivalent in that
they accept the same languages.

We have already considered nondeterminism for both Finite State machines
and Pushdown machines.
For Finite State machines, nondeterminism did not make any
difference in the sense that for any
job done by a nondeterministic Finite State machine there is a deterministic
Finite State machine that can do the same job.
But Pushdown machines are a different
story; there are jobs that can be done by
a nondeterministic Pushdown machine that cannot be done by any
deterministic machine,
one of which is accepting the language of palindromes.

\begin{lemma}
Deterministic and nondeterministic Turing machines solve the same problems.
In particular,
every problem that can be solved by a nondeterministic  machine
can be solved by a deterministic machine.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since a deterministic Turing machine is a special case of a
nondeterministic one, one direction is obvious.

For the other, start with a nondeterministic machine and we will
describe how a deterministic machine can simulate the same computation.
The nondeterministic machine generates a computation tree.
The deterministic machine can do a breadth-first traversal of that tree.
If there is an accepting branch then the deterministic machine will
eventually reach it, and it also will accept the input.
Otherwise, it will not.
\end{proof}

That proof is, basically, time-slicing.
With the machines that are on our desks and in our pockets,
we simulate an unboundedly-parallel computer
by having the CPU switch among processes, giving each enough time to
make some progress but not so much time that other processes are
starved.
This is a form of the dovetailing that we have see earlier.
To the user it appears that many things
are happening at once but actually there is only one, or
at least a limited number of, simultaneous physical processes.\footnote{%
  Depending on how many CPU's the device has.}

So nondeterminism
doesn't add any capacity to what can be computed in principle.
That doesn't mean that these machines are worthless.

For one thing, we saw with nondeterministic Finite State machines
that they were often a good fit for the problems we wanted to solve.
There, we could easily go from problem statement to a nondeterministic machine
and then get a deterministic machine from that.

Similarly, one interesting thing about
nondeterministic Turing machines is that they
appear to be a good match for solving some problems that on a serial machine
are hard to solve.
Take the Travelling Salesman problem.
Stated in guessing terms, the machine just 
guesses a circuit  and then checks whether
it is shorter than the given bound.
So for some problems, they simplify going from the problem statement
to a solution statement.

\subsection{Speed}
But the real excitement is that 
a nondeterministic Turing machine, if we had one,
might be much faster than a deterministic one.
Consider the Satisfiability problem.
Here is an example formula; is it satisfiable?
\begin{equation*}
 F = 
 (P\vee Q)\wedge (P\vee \neg Q)\wedge (\neg P\vee Q)
  \wedge (\neg P\vee \neg Q\vee \neg R) \wedge (\neg P\vee R)
\end{equation*} 
The natural approach uses a truth table
to calculate whether the final column has any $T$'s.
\begin{center} % \definecolor{rowcolor}{gray}{.93} \rowcolors{3}{rcolor}{}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc|ccccc|c}
  $P$  &$Q$  &$R$  &$P\vee Q$ &$P\vee \neg Q$ &$\neg P\vee Q$ &$\neg P\vee \neg Q\vee \neg R$ &$\neg P\vee R$ &$F$ \\ \hline    
  $F$  &$F$  &$F$  &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $F$  &$F$  &$T$  &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $F$  &$T$  &$F$  &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $F$  &$T$  &$T$  &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\[1ex]     
  $T$  &$F$  &$F$  &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$F$ &$F$ \\     
  $T$  &$F$  &$T$  &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ \\     
  $T$  &$T$  &$F$  &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ \\     
  $T$  &$T$  &$T$  &$T$ &$T$ &$T$ &$F$ &$T$ &$F$      
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
This problem seems to be perfect for solving with unbounded parallelism.
The machine could split
into a child process for each line of the
truth table.
These children are done quickly, certainly in polytime,
and then the master process
calls down the bus, ``Any successes?''
(Described in the other way,
the machine guesses a solution, or is given a solution by the demon, and
then only has to verify that it works, which is quick.)
In total then, a nondeterministic machine does this job in polytime
while a serial machine would seem to take
exponential time.

\begin{definition}
A nondeterministic machine
\definend{decides the language~$\lang$ in time $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$} if for any
input $\sigma\in\lang$ the machine computes at least one accepting branch
in time $t(|\sigma|)$. % \footnotemark[2].
\end{definition} % \footnotetext[2]{Whether we use the model that the machine
  % decides by a computation tree or by guessing does not matter.}

So while adding unbounded parallelism to Turing machines doesn't
allow them to compute any entirely new functions, a person could
sensibly conjecture that it may
allow them to compute some of them faster.

However:~no one can prove that.
At this moment, no one can prove that just because
deterministic machines do not come with unbounded parallelism baked in
and must simulate it with time-slicing,
they must be slower.
At this moment, for the deterministic machine case,
(1)~no one has produced a much 
better approach 
than trying all of the lines of the truth table, looking for an output $T$
and (2)~no one can prove that a better way is impossible.

\begin{definition}
The complexity class~\definend{$\compclass{NP}$} is 
the set of languages for which there is a 
a polynomial time nondeterministic Turing machine acceptor.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
$\P\subseteq\NP$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Any deterministic Turing machine is trivially also a nondeterministic one.
So a problem has a polynomial time algorithm on a deterministic
machine trivially has a polytime algorithm on a
nondeterministic machine.
\end{proof}

Again: we don't know which of $\P\subset\NP$ or $\P=\NP$ is true.
This is an extremely important question; we will consider it separately
in the next section.





\subsection{Verifiers}
We are dividing solving a problem into 
producing that solution, and verifying that it is correct.
We are considering the set of problems for which
we can verify, or justify, a `yes' answer in  polytime.
That is, there must be a quick algorithm that,
for each string in the language, gives a proof that
it is in the language.

Contrast such problems with the problem of solving the game of chess.
Like Rock-Paper-Scissors or Tic-Tac-Toe, 
\citetext{chess is a solvable game}{%
  This is Zermelo's Theorem. See \autocite{wiki:ZermelosTheorem}.},
meaning that there exists a strategy
for one of the two players that forces a win, or at least a draw, no
matter what the other player does.
But imagine that a demon hands you a sheaf of papers and tells you
that it contains a perfect chess strategy.
If you tried to verify it by having a computer step through
the responses to each move and responses to those
responses, etc., then even having the strategy in your hand, 
verifying that it is correct seems
beyond the Earth's computing capacity.
The possibility tree is just too big.



\begin{definition}
A deterministic Turing machine $\TM$ is a
\definend{verifier of a language} if 
for every $\sigma\in\kleenestar{\B}$, that string is member of the language
if and only if
there exists a \definend{witness} or \definend{certificate}
string $\omega\in\kleenestar{\B}$ such that the machine
$\TM$ accepts the input $\sequence{\sigma,\omega}$.
\end{definition}

The definition does not say where the witness~$\omega$ comes from.
In some sense this formalizes the idea of a demon providing the
answer and $\TM$'s sole job is to verify its correctness.

Notice also that the definition does not say that when the string is not
in the language then there is a witness to that.

% The next result is that the two formulations, guesser and
% verifier, are equivalent up to some polynomial sloppiness.

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:VerifierAndGuesserEquiv}
A language is a member of $\NP$, 
it is accepted by a polynomial time nondeterministic Turing machine,
if and only
if it can be verified by some polynomial
time deterministic verifier.
\end{lemma}

The verifier must run in time polynomial in the length of the input~$\sigma$.
This implies is that the length of the witness~$\lh{\omega}$
is polynomial in~$\sigma$, because the verifier is a 
Turing machine and reads one bit at a time so if 
the witness were too long then the
machine would not be able to even read it in the alotted time.

Observe that taking the polynomial~$p$ to be trivial $p(i)=0$
gives that $\compclass{P}\subseteq\compclass{NP}$.

\begin{proof}
The idea is that the sequence of choices made through nondeterminism
can be bound together to make the witness, and vice versa.

Suppose first that the language~$\lang$ is accepted by the nondeterministic
Turing machine~$\TM$ in polynomial time;
we will construct a polynomial time verifier~$\TM[V]$.
Let $\map{p}{\N}{\N}$ be the polynomial 
such that the time taken by~$\TM$ on input~$\sigma\in\kleenestar{\B}$
is $p(|\sigma|)$.
For any $\sigma\in\lang$ the computation tree has an accepting branch.
We can make a certificate $\omega$ of the branchings.
With the certificate a deterministic verifier can retrace $\TM$'s
accepting branch.
Because
the branch's length must be less than the length of the computation,
$p(|\sigma|)$,
the certificate will have length less than $p(|\sigma|)$.
So the verifier~$\TM[V]$ can do the retracing in polynomial time.

For the other direction suppose that the language $\lang$
is accepted by a verifier~$\TM[V]$ that runs in time
that is bounded by some polynomial~$p$.
We will describe a process in guessing terms.
Given $\sigma\in\kleenestar{\B}$, produce a sequence of $|\sigma|$
guesses of branches in the computation tree to make a witness string~$\omega$.
Use this witness along with $\sigma$ as inputs to $\TM[V]$.
Because of the verifier step, there is a way for this guessing process to
succeed if and only if $\sigma\in\lang$.
Because the verifier runs in polynomial time, the entire process of guessing and
then verifying does also.
\end{proof}

Thus, a problem is in NP if and only if we can provide a
polynomially long proof witness for any
positive solution.
So, one way to think of $\NP$ is that it is the class of problems which
have efficient verifiers: there is a polytime algorithm that,
given a solution~$\omega$,
can verify that it is is correct.

Often in mathematics we have a definition that captures the concept, followed 
by a result that makes the definition easier to work with.
This is an example.
While the description of a language as accepted by a polynomial time
nondeterministic Turing machine is conceptually cleaner, experience shows that
in practice the verifier description is typically easier.
The following examples illustrate.

\begin{example}
The Factorization problem is a member of the
class~$\compclass{NP}$.
Given a number~$n\in\N$, we can usee as witness~$\omega$ some 
$a$ such that $1<a<n$ and $a$ is a factor of~$n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The Satisfiability problem is a member of~$\compclass{NP}$.
Given a propositional logic formula, we can use as a witness~$\omega$
the line number of an input that satisfies the formula.
Making a deterministic Turing machine that
runs in polynomial time that, given the formula and the witness,
verifies in polynomial time that the formula is satisfied is routine.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The Travelling Salesman problem is in the class~$\compclass{NP}$.
Given a graph~$\mathcal{G}$ and a bound~$B$, 
we could use as a witness a sequence of nodes that
constitute a circuit of total cost less than the bound
$\omega=\sequence{v_{i_0},v_{i_1},\ldots v_{i_k}}$.
We can easily write a deterministic Turing machine that, given
the graph and such a witness, verifies that the circuit in this
graph costs less than the bound. 
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The Subset Sum problem is in~$\compclass{NP}$.
Given a set $S$ of numbers and a target sum~$B$,
we can use as the witness~$\omega$ a subset of~$S$ whose members add to the
target.
Then we can easily write
a deterministic routine that inputs $\sequence{S,B}$ and $\omega$
and verifies that the subset adds to the target, in polynomial time.
\end{example}

In summary, we are interested in knowing:~for which problems
are there relatively good algorithms, and for which are there not?
Cobham's Thesis is that a problem in~$\P$ has a good algorithm.
In this section we considered a class of problems for which
there is a good way to verify solutions, but we don't know whether that
is enough to mean it has a good algorithm to give a solution.

\section{Problem reduction}
% "Reduction decreases the number of problems without increasing the number of
% answers"
A \definend{reduction}
is a way to translate from one problem to another.
When we studied incomputability the problems were decision problems for
sets.
There we defined Turing reductions, where $B\leq_T A$
if there is a computable function~$f$ such that
$x\in B$ if and only if $f(x)\in A$.
This is a translation between problems\Dash a
method to answer questions about~$B$ can be used
to answer questions about~$A$.
That explains the terminology 'problem~$A$ reduces to problem~$B$'.

Computational complexity works with language decision problems.
Here problem~$\lang_1$ is reducible to problem~$\lang_0$
if a method for solving~$\lang_0$ efficiently
could be used to solve problem~$\lang_1$ efficiently.

\begin{definition}
The language $\lang_1$ is
\definend{polynomial time reducible}
(or \definend{polynomial time mapping reducible}, or
\definend{polynomial time many-one reducible}, or
\definend{Karp reducible})
to the language~$\lang_0$, written $\lang_1\leq_p\lang_0$,
if there is a computable function
$\map{f}{\kleenestar{\B}}{\kleenestar{\B}}$ that runs in polynomial time
such that
$\sigma\in\lang_1$ if and only if~$f(\sigma)\in\lang_0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
If $\prob_1\leq_p\prob_0$ and $\prob_0\in\P$ then $\prob_1\in\P$. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In a polynomial reduction, if there is an algorithm for solving
$\lang_0$ in $\bigOh(n^i)$ and the polynomial has degree~$j$
then the solution to $\lang_1$ that is $\bigOh(n^{i+j})$ is:~given
input $\sigma$, find $f(\sigma)$ and then apply the algorithm
to determine if it is a member of $\lang_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{fig}[fig:ProblemBeanWithReductions]{The bean encloses all problems.
  The ones with fast algorithms are at the bottom.
  Problems are connected if the one underneath reduces to the
  one above.}
  \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp01.pdf}}
  \hspace{0.6em}
  \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp00.pdf}}
\end{fig}


\begin{example}
We will sketch an argument that Graph Colorability
reduces to Satisfiability,
that $\text{Graph Colorability}\leq_p\SAT$.
We'll go through the $k=3$ construction; other $k$'s go the same way.

Recall that a graph is $k$-colorable if we can partition the vertices
into $k$~many classes, called `colors' because that's how they
are pictured, so that there is no edge between two same-colored
vertices.
\begin{animatedfigure}{A $3$-coloring of \protect\citetext{a graph}{This is the Petersen graph, a rich source of counterexamples for conjectures in Graph Theory}.}\centering 
  \animategraphics[poster=last]{2}{complexity/asy/complexity/complexity}{00}{01}
\end{animatedfigure} 
\noindent And, a propositional logic statement~$S$ is satisfiable if there is an
assignment of the variables in the statement that make the statement
as a whole evaluate to~$T$.

To translate between these two we must give a function~$f$
that inputs a
particular $3$-coloring problem~$\lang_0$
and outputs a problem of the satisfiability of a particular statement
in propositional logic, $f(\mathcal{\lang_0})=\lang_1$,
such that the statement is satisfiable
if and only if the graph is $3$-colorable.
More precisely, the function inputs the graph's
bitstring representation
and outputs the statement's bitstring representation, and must run in
polynomial time.

This translation~$f$ inputs a graph~$\mathcal{G}$
with $\set{v_0,\ldots\, v_{n-1}}$.
The statement will have $3n$-many Boolean variables:
$
  {a_0,\ldots a_{n-1}}
$, and
$  
  {b_0,\ldots b_{n-1}}
$, and
$  
  {c_0,\ldots c_{n-1}}
$.
The idea is that if the $i$-th vertex~$v_i$ gets the first color then $a_i=T$,
while if it gets the second color then $b_i=T$, and if it gets the
third color then $c_i=T$.
For each vertex~$v_i$ create a clause saying that it gets at least one color.
\begin{equation*}
  (a_i\vee b_i \vee c_i)
\end{equation*}
In addition, for each edge~$\set{v_i,v_j}$
create three clauses that together ensure that the edge does not connect
two same-color vertices.
\begin{equation*}
  (\neg a_i\vee \neg a_j)
  \qquad
  (\neg b_i\vee \neg b_j)
  \qquad
  (\neg c_i\vee \neg c_j)
\end{equation*}
Now the statement is the conjunction of those clauses;
it joins them with $\wedge$'s.
The graph has a $3$-coloring if and only if that statement
is satisfiable.

This illustrates.
The graph has four vertices so the statement starts with four clauses,
saying that for each vertex~$v_i$ at least one of the associated
variables $a_i$, $b_i$, or~$c_i$ has the value~$T$.
The graph has four edges, $v_0v_1$, $v_0v_3$, $v_1v_2$, and
$v_2v_3$.
The statement continues with three clauses for each edge, 
together ensuring that the variables associated with the edge's vertices
do not both have the value~$T$.

Completing the proof means
checking that the translation function, which inputs a bitstring
representation of~$\mathcal{G}$ and outputs a bitstring represenation

This argument is messy but routine.

\end{example}

The significance of the reduction is that we now know that
if we could solve~$\SAT$ in polynomial time then we could solve
$k$-colorability in polynomial time.
Given a $k$-coloring problem~$\lang_0$, translate it to a
a $\SAT$ problem~$f(\lang_0)=\lang_1$.
The translation takes polytime and~$\lang_1$ takes polytime, so
in total this is a polytime scheme.

That example give some sense of why $\SAT$ can be a convenient
problem to reduce to, since describing the conditions in a problem
in logical statement terms is natural.
Another kind of natural reduction is when we can see that one problem
as a special case of another, or at least closely related.

\begin{example}
We will show that $\text{Subset Sum}\leq_p\text{Knapsack}$,
so that a quick way to solve the Knapsack problem would
yield a quick way to solve the Subset Sum problem.

Recall that the Subset Sum problem
starts with a set of numbers $S=\set{b_0,\ldots b_{k-1}}$
and a target~$T$.
It asks if there is a subset whose elements add to the target.
As an illustration, one instance~$\lang_0$ is to ask if there is a subset of
$S=\set{18, 23, 31, 33, 72,  86, 94}$ that adds to $T=126$.

The Knapsack problem starts with a set
of objects~$\hat{S}=\set{s_0, \ldots s_{n-1}}$
and two bounds $W,V\in\N$.
Each~$s_i$ has both a weight~$w(s_i)\in\N^+$ and a value~$v(s_i)\in\N^+$.
The problem is to decide if there is subset $K\subseteq S$
such that the sum of the element weights is less than or equal to~$W$
while the sum of the values is greater than or equal to~$V$.
For the reduction consider the special case is where each element
has the same weight as value, $w(s_i)=v(s_i)$,
and where $W=V$.
In this case subset $K$ satisfies the conditions only if the sum of the
weights and values exactly equals the limit and target.

Following the above illustration,
from the Subset Sum instance
produce the Knapsack instance $\lang_1=f(\lang_0)$ 
by defining a set of objects~$\hat{S}$ with the same number of elements as~$S$,
letting the weight and value of each element equal the associated
element of~$S$,
and setting the weight and value targets $W,V$ to equal~$T$.

  \tabulartext{value}  &$18$  &$23$  &$31$  &$33$  &$72$  &$86$ &$94$  \\
\end{tabular}

$W=V=126$
\end{center}
There is a solution to the Subset Sum instance~$\lang_0$ if and only
if there is a solution to the Knapsack instance~$\lang_1$.
Seeing that the function~$f$ to convert a bitstring representation
of $\lang_0$ to one of~$\lang_1$ is polytime is easy.
\end{example}

is at least as hard as every problem in $\NP$.

\subsection{$\compclass{NP}$ complete}
\margingraphic{0.18\textwidth}{complexity/pix/cook.jpg}{\label{fig:StephenCook}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Cook}{Stephen Cook b~1939}} % http://web.cs.toronto.edu/Assets/Computer+Science+Digital+Assets/cooknsercmed.jpg http://web.cs.toronto.edu/news/current/herzbergcook.htm
Because $\P\subseteq\NP$, the class $\NP$ contains lots of easy problems,
ones with a fast algorithm.
For instance, one member of $\NP$ is
the problem of determining whether a number is odd.
Of course, the interest in the class is that it also contains lots of
problems that seem to be hard.
Can we prove that they are hard?

In 1971 S~Cook raised this question.
He noted the significance of polynomial time reducibility, so that
an efficient solution to one problem results in an efficient
solution for the other.
And, he
showed that among the problems in $\NP$,
there are ones that are maximally hard.\footnote{%
  This was also shown by L~Levin who was behind the Iron Curtain so
  that knowledge of his work did not spread for some time.}

\begin{theorem}[Cook-Levin Theorem]
The Satisfiability problem has the property
that $\prob\leq_p\SAT$ for any $\prob\in\NP$.
\end{theorem}

First, $\SAT\in\NP$ because
a nondeterministic machine can guess which line of the truth
table to verify.
Said another way:~given a Boolean formula,
use as a certificate a sequence giving an assignment of truth values
that satisfies the formula.

\margingraphic{0.18\textwidth}{complexity/pix/levin.png}{\label{fig:L Levin}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Levin}{Leonid Levin b~1948}} % https://www.bu.edu/today/2014/american-academy-of-arts-sciences-taps-cas-prof-for-membership/
The rest of the proof is long so it is in an Extra section.
But its basic idea is reachable.
We are given a problem $\prob\in\NP$ and we must show that
$\prob\leq_p\SAT$.
To show that $\prob$ is reducible to $\SAT$ we must translate
membership questions for $\prob$ into a Boolean
formulas, in such a way that the membership answer is `yes' if and
only if the formula yields $T$.
The only thing we know about the problem $\prob$ is that its input
$\sigma$ is
accepted by some nondeterministic machine~$\TM$ in time given by
a polynomial~$p$.
So the proof constructs, from $\TM$, $\sigma$, and $p$,
a Boolean formula that yields $T$ if and only if
$\TM$ accepts~$\sigma$.
The Boolean formula encodes the constraints that a Turing machine
operates under, such as that the only tape symbol that can be changed in
any one step is the one under the machine's I/O head.

Our main interest in the theorem is that it shows there are 
problems in $\NP$ that are at least as hard as any problem in
that class.

\begin{definition}
A problem~$\prob$ is \definend{hard for a class~\compclass{C}} if 
all problems in that class reduce to it:
if $\hat{\prob}\in\compclass{C}$ then $\hat{\prob}\leq_p\prob$.
A problem is \definend{complete for a class} if it is hard for that
class and is also a member of that class.
\end{definition}

The special case of $\NP$~completeness is worth its own definition,
for reference.

\begin{definition}
A problem~$\prob$ is \definend{$\NP$-hard}
if $\hat{\prob}\leq_p\prob$ for every~$\hat{\prob}\in\compclass{NP}$.
A problem~$\prob$ is \citetext{\definend{$\NP$~complete}}{%
  The name came from a contest run by Knuth; see \url{http://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2010/11/by-any-other-name-would-be-just-as-hard.html}.}
if it is $\NP$-hard
and it is a member of $\NP$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:InteractionNPCompleteAndReduction}
If $\prob_0$ is $\NP$~complete, and $\prob_0\leq_p\prob_1$,
and $\prob_1\in\NP$ then $\prob_1$ is $\NP$~complete.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{exer:ProveInteractionNPCompleteAndReduction}.
\end{proof}

The $\NP$~complete problems are to the class~$\NP$ as the problems Turing-equivalent to~$K$,
the solution to the \HP, 
are to the computably enumerable sets.
They are at the top level of their class.

\margingraphic{0.20\textwidth}{complexity/pix/karp.jpg}{\label{fig:RichardKarp}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_M._Karp}{Richard M.\@ Karp b~1935}} % https://simons.berkeley.edu/people/richard-karp  https://simons.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/styles/profile_main/public/rk1.jpg?itok=1kn5XodZ
Soon after Cook raised the question, R~Karp brought it to widespread attention.
He produced a list of twenty one problems that were well known to be difficult,
ones that many smart and hard-working people had for years
attacked, and had been unable find an efficient algorithm.
Karp showed that these problems were all $\NP$~complete and so were
equivalent in that if any one could be solved by an efficient algorithm
then they all could be. % Based off Karp's Turing award statement http://amturing.acm.org/award_winners/karp_3256708.cfm

Karp's paper made clear
that the collection of $\NP$~complete problems encompassed many
hard problems, spread across 
Computer Science, Mathematics, and the natural sciences.
By now many thousands of such problems have been proved to be $\NP$~complete. 
Whatever it is that makes these problems hard, they all share.
 
\citetext{The next list}{%
  These are from the standard, classic, reference \autocite{GareyJohnson}.}
contains the problems
most often used to
prove that something is $\NP$~complete.
These descriptions appeared earlier; they are copied
here for convenience.
Typically, proving that a problem~$\prob$ is $\NP$~complete has two halves.
The first half shows that~$\prob\in\NP$.
Usually this is easy, just show that a witness exists that could
be verified in polytime.
The second half shows that your problem is $\NP$-hard.
Often this involves showing that
some problem on the list reduces to~$\prob$; for instance, that 
$\text{$3$-$\SAT$}\leq_p\prob$.

\begin{theorem}[Basic $\compclass{NP}$ Complete Problems]
Each of these problems is $\compclass{NP}$-complete.
\begin{thmdescription}
\item[$3$-Satisfiability, $3$-$\SAT$]
Given a propositional logic formula in conjunctive normal form
in which each clause has at most $3$ variables,
decide if it is satisfiable.

\item[$3$-dimensional matching]
Given as input a set $M\subseteq X\times Y\times Z$, where the sets $X,Y,Z$
all have the same number of elements, $n$,
decide if there is a matching,
a set $\hat{M}\subseteq M$ containing~$n$ elements such that no
two of the triples in $\hat{M}$ agree on any of their coordinates.

\item[Vertex cover]
Given a graph and a bound~$B\in\N$,
decide if the graph has a $B$-vertex cover,
a size~$B$ set of vertices~$C$
such that for any edge $v_iv_j$, at least one of its ends
is a member of~$C$.

\item[Clique]
Given a graph and a bound~$B\in\N$,
decide if the graph has a $B$-clique,
a set of $B$-many vertices such that any two are connected.

\item[Hamiltonian circuit]
Given a graph, decide if it contains a Hamiltonian circuit,
a cyclic path that includes each vertex.

\item[Partition]
Given a finite set~$A$ that has for each of its elements
an associated size $s(a)\in\N^+$,
decide if there is a division of the set into two halves
$\hat{A},A-\hat{A}$ so the total of the sizes is the same in
both halves, $\sum_{a\in\hat{A}}s(a)=\sum_{a\notin\hat{A}}s(a)$.
\end{thmdescription}
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
The Travelling Salesman problem is $\NP$ complete because
the Hamiltonian Circuit problem
reduces to it:
$\text{Hamiltonian Circuit}\leq_p\text{Travelling Salesman}$.
We must produce a reduction function~$f$ and check that it is polytime.

The function inputs an instance of the Hamiltonian Circuit problem, a graph
$\mathcal{G}=\sequence{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}}$
whose edges are unweighted.
It returns
the instance of the Travelling Salesman problem that uses the vertex set
$\mathcal{V}$ as cities and that takes the distances between the cities
to be: $d(v_i,v_j)=1$ if $v_iv_j$ is an edge of $\mathcal{G}$ and
$d(v_i,v_j)=2$ if $v_iv_j\notin\mathcal{E}$,
and such that the bound~$B$ is the number of vertices~$|\mathcal{V}|$.

The bound means that there will be a Travelling Salesman solution
if and only if there is a Hamiltonian Circuit solution, namely
the Travelling Salesman solution uses the edges of the Hamiltonian Circuit.
What remains is to show that $f$ is polytime in the size of the input problem
instance, which we take to be the number of vertices since the number of
edges is less than twice the number of vertices (so that polytime in the
number of vertices is also polytime in the number of vertices plus the
number of edges).
To output the Travelling Salesman problem instance, the algorithm examines
all the pairs of vertices, which is a nested loop and so takes time that
is quadratic in the number of vertices.
\end{example}

One of Karp's points was the practical importance of
$\NP$~completeness.
Many problems from applications fall into this class.
The next example illustrates.
(It also illustrates that reductions can be complex.)

\begin{example} % From https://people.cs.umass.edu/~barring/cs311/disc/9.html
Most colleges first make up a schedule by putting classes into
time slots and then students pick which classes they will take.
Imagine instead that students first select the classes, and then the
college must solve the problem of deciding if those classes have a
non-conflicting time schedule.

Consider a college with $2$~time slots,
$k$-many classes, each with some maximum capacity, and 
$n$-many students.
Every student~$s$ has two disjoint lists of classes
$\ell_s,\hat{\ell}_s$ and
must choose one class from each list $c_s\in\ell_s$ and
$\hat{c}_s\in\hat{\ell}_s$.
The problem~$\prob$ is to decide if there is a way to partition the set
of classes into two such that every class is in a time slot and every
student is in both of the classes they selected, and no student is in
two classes in the same time slot.
We will show that $\prob$ is $\NP$~complete.

Observe that $\prob$ is a member of $\NP$ because checking a solution is
fast:~a witness~$\omega$ is a sequence of assignments of students to classes
and classes to time slots, which has length polynomial
in the number of students, and we can in polytime
check that each student is assigned one class from each of their lists,
that each class is given a time slot, etc.

We will show $\prob$ is $\NP$-hard by showing that
$\text{$3$-$\SAT$}\leq_p\prob$, that
there is a reduction function that runs in polytime.
An input instance to this function
consists of $m$-many clauses that are joined by $\wedge$'s,
each clause with $3$ or fewer of the Boolean variables
$x_0$, \ldots $x_{n-1}$ or their negations,
joined by $\vee$'s.
The reduction function gives as output an instance of $\prob$.
The output instance will have a successful schedule if and only if the
input formula is satisfiable.

For each of the input instance's Boolean variables
the output instance will have an associated course: $c_0$, \ldots $c_{n-1}$
(of capacity equal to the number of students).
There will be more courses also, including one named `T' and one named `F'
(the idea is that
if the input is satisfied by an assignment where $x_i=T$ then
the output can allot $c_i$ to the same slot as course~T).
These will each have capacity $2m+1$.
Also start by giving the output $\prob$ instance 
a student $s_0$ whose two lists are
$\ell_{s_0}={\text{T}}$ and $\hat{\ell}_{s_0}=\set{\text{F}}$; this
puts them in separate slots.

Now consider the clauses in the input formula.
The easiest clauses are the ones without negations, of the form
$x_{i_0}$, or $x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}$, or $x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}\vee x_{i_2}$.
If the clause is $x_{i_0}$ then in the output instance we create a student
$s$ with the two lists
$\ell_s=\set{c_{i_0}}$ and $\hat{\ell}_s=\set{\text{F}}$.
If the clause is $x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}$ then the student
gets
$\ell_s=\set{c_{i_0},c_{i_1}}$ and $\hat{\ell}_s=\set{\text{F}}$.
Similarly, for $x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}\vee x_{i_2}$ the created student's
lists are 
$\ell_s=\set{c_{i_0},c_{i_1},c_{i_2}}$ and $\hat{\ell}_s=\set{\text{F}}$.

Clauses with only negations, of the form
$\neg x_{i_0}$, or $\neg x_{i_0}\vee \neg x_{i_1}$,
or $\neg x_{i_0}\vee \neg x_{i_1}\vee \neg x_{i_2}$,
are similar.
For instance, for $\neg x_{i_0}$ we create a student with lists 
$\ell_s=\set{\text{T}}$ and $\hat{\ell}_s=\set{c_{i_0}}$.

The messiest clauses have a mixture of positive and negative atoms.
We will do an example: suppose that the $j$-th clause is
$x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}\vee \neg x_{i_2}$.
In addition to the classes $c_{i_0}$, $c_{i_1}$, and $c_{i_2}$ created earlier,
we also create three new classes $t_j$, $f_j$ and~$z_j$, with
capacity of one student each.
And we don't just create one student, we create three: $s_j$, $u_j$, and $v_j$.
Here are their lists.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{r|cc}
  \multicolumn{1}{r}{\tabulartext{Student}}
    &$\ell$                        &$\hat{\ell}$  \\
  \hline
  $s_j$                  &$\set{c_{i_0},c_{i_1},t_j}$    &$\set{c_{i_2},f_j}$  \\
  $u_j$                  &$\set{t_j,z_j}$               &$\set{\text{F}}$  \\
  $v_j$                  &$\set{\text{T}}$              &$\set{f_j,z_j}$  
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

To confirm that this reduction function suffices,
we must show that if there is an assignment of truth values to the
Boolean variables that satisfies the formula then 
there is a course schedule solution, and we must also show the converse.

So first suppose that there a way to give each Boolean variable a value of
$T$ or $F$ such that the entire propositional logic formula evaluates
to~$T$.
Then we get a non-conflicting course time slot alottment by:
where $x_i=T$ put the course~$c_i$
in the same time slot as T,
and where $x_j=F$ put the course~$c_j$ in the F slot.
This clearly works for the all-positive atom or all-negative atoms cases.
The interesting cases are the mixed postive and negative atom clauses, such as
$x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}\vee \neg x_{i_2}$.
% For the input propositional logic formula to evaluate to $T$ this
% clause itself must evaluate to $T$.
We can check that for each possible assignment making this clause
evaluate to $T$ there is a non-conflicting way to arrange the other
courses.
For instance, suppose that
$x_{i_0}=T$, $x_{i_1}=F$, and $x_{i_2}=T$.
We have put course~$c_{i_0}$ in the time slot when~T is offered and
student~$s_j$ can take it then, and similarly can also take
course~$c_{i_2}$ when F is offered.
Then student~$u_j$ takes~$t_j$ when T is offered and also takes
course~F, and student~$v_j$ takes T and~$f_j$.

Conversely, suppose that for any assignment of truth values to
the Boolean variables the input formula is not satisfied.
We will show that implies that for any allocation of classes into time slots
there is a conflict.

So fix an allocation of classes into time slots, say with
the classes $c_{i_0}$, $c_{i_1}$ ,\ldots{} being offered in the
same slot as class~T and
the rest with class~F.
Associate with that allocation of classes the assignment of Boolean variables
that sets $x_{i_0}=T$, $x_{i_1}=T$, etc.
and the rest to~$F$.
This assignment causes the formula to yield a value of~$F$,
as this formula is not satisfiable.
So for this assignment the formula has some clause, clause~$j$, that does not
evaluate to~$T$.

The first possibility is that clause~$j$ has either all positive atoms
or all negative atoms.
Foe example, let the clause be $x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}\vee x_{i_2}$.
Then the assignment must have
$x_{i_0}=F$, $x_{i_1}=F$, and $x_{i_2}=F$, and so the allocation of classes
must be that all three of $c_{i_0}$, $c_{i_1}$, and $c_{i_2}$ go with class~F.
This will cause a conflict in course assignments because the output instance
was created with a student~$s_j$ having the lists
$\ell_{s_j}=\set{c_{i_0}, c_{i_1}, c_{i_2}}$ and $\hat{\ell}_{s_j}=\set{F}$.

The other case is that clause~$j$ has a mixed form, such as
$x_{i_0}\vee x_{i_1}\vee \neg x_{i_2}$, so that the Boolean variable
assignment is
$x_{i_0}=F$, $x_{i_1}=F$, and $x_{i_2}=T$.
This association comes from the allocation of classes that puts 
$c_{i_0}$ and $c_{i_1}$ with class~F, and puts $c_{i_2}$ with~T.
We claim that there is no non-conflicting way to assign students to courses.
Refer to the table above.
This allocation of classes could only be non-conflicting if student~$s_j$
selected classes~$t_j$ and~$f_j$.
But then because of the capacity of one in these classes,
student~$u_j$ must select~$z_j$ and~$F$, and student~$v_j$
has a conflict. 

Whew!
To close, observe that the reduction function that creates the
output $\prob$ instance from the input propositional logic formula
runs in polytime.
\end{example}

Another way to use the basic list
to show that a problem~$\prob$ is $\NP$~complete
is to find a special case of~$\prob$ on that list.

\begin{example}
The Knapsack problem starts with a 
set~$=\set{s_0,\ldots s_{k-1}}$ whose
elements
have a weight~$w(s_i)\in\N^+$ and a value~$v(s_i)\in\N^+$, and a
weight bound $B\in\N^+$ and value target~$T\in\N^+$.
It asks if there is
$K\subseteq S$ whose elements have total weight less than or equal to the
bound and total value greater than or equal to the target.

This is $\NP$~complete.
The witness~$\omega$, where $\omega[i]=1$ if $s_i$ is in
the knapsack, and $\omega[i]=0$ if it is not, is only $k$~bits long and so is
$\bigOh(n)$ in the number of elements in $S$.
A deterministic machine can verify this witness in polynomial
time since it only has to total the weights and values
of the elements of~$K$.

To show that it is $\NP$-hard, we show that a special case
is $\NP$-hard.
Consider the case where $w(s_i)=v(s_i)$ for all~$s_i\in S$, and where
$B$ and~$K$ each equal half of the total of all the weights.
This is a Partition problem, which is in the above list.
\end{example}

  \includegraphics[scale=3]{complexity/xkcdnpcomplete}
\end{fig}

There are problems that are members of $\NP$ but are not
$\NP$~complete.
Most experts believe that while the Factoring problem is hard for classical
computers\footnote{%
  In 1994, P~Shor discovered an algorithm for a quantum computer that
  solves the Factoring problem in polynomial time.
  This will have significant implications if quantum computation proves
  to be possible to engineer.}
but that it is not $\compclass{NP}$~complete.
Experts also suspect that the Graph Isomorphism is not $\NP$ complete.  
As always with such judgements, though, without proof they could turn out
to be mistaken.

\subsection{$\EXP$}
In this chapter's first section we included in the list of
commonly encountered orders of growth the two $\bigOh(2^n)$
and~$\bigOh(3^n)$, and by extension other exponential orders.

\begin{definition}
A problem is an element of the complexity class $\EXP$ if there is an
algorithm for solving it that runs in time $\bigOh(b^{p(n)})$ for some
constant base~$b$ and polynomial~$p$.
\end{definition}

The Satisfiability problem can be solved in exponential time
by checking each row of the truth table, and any $\compclass{NP}$ problem
can be solved from the Satisfiability problem with only an addition of
polytime.
So $\EXP$ has this relationship to the classes we have already studied.

\begin{lemma}
$\P\subseteq\NP\subseteq\EXP$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\prob\in\NP$.
We can verify $\prob$ on a deterministic Turing machine~$\TM$
in polynomial time using a witness whose length is bounded by the
same polynomial.
Let this problem's bound be $n^c$. 

We will decide $\prob$ in exponential time by brute-forcing it:~we
will use~$\TM$ to run every possible verification. 
Trying any single witness requires polynomial time, $n^c$.
Witnesses are in binary so for length~$\ell$ there
are $\sum_{0\leq i\leq\ell}2^{i}=2^{\ell+1}-1$~many possibile ones;
In total then, brute force requires $\bigOh(n^c 2^{n^c})$ operations.
Finish by observing that $n^c 2^{n^c}$ is in $\bigOh(2^{n^c})$. 
\end{proof}

We don't know whether there are any $\compclass{NP}$ problems that
absolutely require exponential time.
Conceivably $\compclass{NP}$ is contained in a smaller determinstic
time complexity class\Dash maybe the Satisfiability problem, for instance,
can be solved in less than exponential time.
But we just don't know.

   They are drawn with strict containment, which most experts guess

Whereas a first take on 
polytime is ``can conceivably be used,'' 
a first approximation of
$\EXP$ is that it contains
problems whose best algorithms are too slow to be used.

That is, $\EXP$ contains nearly every problem, and every class, that
we concern ourselves with in practice.
We can construct theories about still harder problems, but
$\EXP$ is big enough to contain most problems
we ever hope to attack.

\subsection{$\P=\NP$?}
Every deterministic Turing machine is, trivially, a nondeterministic machine
and so $\P\subseteq\NP$.
What about the other direction?

Posing $\P$~versus~$\NP$ 
expresses the question:~Does adding parallelism add speed?
Can unbounded parallelism bring problems
from super-polynomial to polynomial?

The short answer is that no one knows.
One of these two pictures is right but
we do not know which one.
\begin{fig}[fig:ProblemBeansPNP]{Which is it: $\P\subset\NP$
  or $\P=\NP$?}
  % \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp01.pdf}}
  % \hspace{0.6em}
  \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp04.pdf}}
  \hspace{5em}
  \vcenteredhbox{\includegraphics{complexity/asy/pnp/pnp05.pdf}}
\end{fig}
\noindent There is a simple way
to settle the question.
By \cref{lem:InteractionNPCompleteAndReduction}, if someone shows
that any $\NP$ complete problem is a member of~$\P$ then
$\P=\NP$.
If someone shows that there is an $\NP$ problem that is not a member
of~$\P$ then $\P\neq\NP$.
But despite nearly a half century of effort by many extremely bright
people, no one has been able to do either one.

A great many people have worked on this question because this
is an extremely important problem.\footnote{%
  One indication of its importance is that it is on a list
  of problems in mathematics for which there is a one million dollar prize;
  see \protect\url{http://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems}.}
True, as formulated in Karp's original paper, the question of whether
$\P$ equals $\NP$ may seem of only technical interest.
\begin{quotation}
  \citetext{A large class}{%
  See \autocite{Karp}.}
  of computational problems involve the
  determination of properties of graphs, digraphs, integers,
  arrays of integers, finite families of finite sets, boolean formulas and
  elements of other countable domains.
  Through simple encodings \ldots{} these problems can be converted into
  language recognition problems, and we can inquire into their
  computational complexity.
  It is reasonable to consider such a problem satisfactorily solved when
  an algorithm for its solution is found which terminates within a
  number of steps bounded by a polymomial in the length of the input.
  We show that a large number of classic unsolved problems of covering,
  matching, packing, routing, assignment and sequencing are equivalent,
  in the sense that either each of them possesses a
  polynomial-bounded algorithm or none of them does.
\end{quotation}
But Karp demonstrated that many of the problems people have been struggling
with in practical applications fall into this category.
Researchers who have been struggling with 
a Vertex Cover problem and those who have been
struggling with a Clique problem
found that they are in some sense working on the same problem.
By now the list of these problems includes
determining the best layout of transistors on a chip,
developing accurate financial-forecasting models,
analyzing protein-folding behavior in a cell,
or finding the most energy-efficient airplane wing.

In practice, proving that a problem is a member of $\NP$ is often an
ending point; a researcher may well reason that continuing to
try to find a algorithm is likely to be not fruitful since many of the
best minds of mathematics, science, and computer science have failed
at it.
They may instead turn their attention elsewhere, perhaps to
approximations.


In the book's first part we studied problems that are unsolvable.
That was a black and white situation; either a problem is solvable
in principle or it is unsolvable, even
in principle.
We here study the possibility that many problems of practical interest
are infeasible.
That is, it may be that 
the class of $\compclass{NP}$ complete problems form
a kind of transition between 
possible and impossible.

We can use this to engineering advantage;
for instance, schemes for holding votes are notoriously prone to
manipulation and there are theorems that say they must be.
But we can hope to use system that, while in principle
are manipulatable, are constructed so that it is infeasible to
compute how to do that.
Another example of the same thing is the celebrated
RSA encryption system that is used to protect Internet
commerce; see~\cref{extra:RSA}.  

This discussion
returns us to the book's opening question about
mathematical proof.
Recall the \textit{Entscheidungsproblem} that was a motivation behind
the definition of a Turing machine.
% It was proposed in 1928 by Hilbert and Ackermann.
It asks for
an algorithm that inputs a mathematical statement~$\sigma$
and outputs whether $\sigma$ is true.
Maybe it is a \citetext{caricature}{%
  Paul Erdős joked that a mathematician is a machine for turning
  coffee into theorems.}
but imagine that the job of mathematicians is to prove theorems.
The \textit{Entscheidungsproblem} asks if we can replace them with machines.

In the intervening century we have come to understand, through
the work of G\"odel and others,
the difference between a statement's being true and its being provable,
and that we cannot always decide from axioms whether a statement is true.
Consequently, we modify
the \textit{Entscheidungsproblem} to ask for an algorithm
that inputs statements and decides whether they are provable.

The plan for that is simple, in principle.
A  proof is a sequence of statements,
$\sigma_0$, $\sigma_1$, \ldots, $\sigma_k$,
where the final statement is the conclusion,
and each statement either is an axiom or
else follows from the statements before it
by an application
of a rule of logic (a typical rule allows the simultaneous replacement of all
$x$'s with $y+4$'s).
In principle a computer could brute-force the question of
whether a given statement is provable by
doing a breadth-first search of all derivations.
If a proof exists, it will appear eventually.

The difficulty is that final word, eventually.
This algorithm is very slow.
So we ask:~Is there a tractable way?

In the terminology we now have,
the modified \textit{Entscheidungsproblem} is a decision problem:~given
a statement~$\sigma$ and bound~$B\in\N$, we ask if there is 
a sequence of statements witnessing a proof ending in~$\sigma$
that is shorter than the bound. 
A computer can quickly check whether a given proof is valid.
So with the current status of the
$\P$ versus~$\NP$ problem,
the answer to the question in the prior paragraph is that
no one knows of one but no one can show there isn't one either.

As far back as 1956, G\"odel raised these issues in a letter to
von~Neumann (this letter did not become public until years later).
\begin{quotation}\small
One can obviously easily construct a Turing machine,
which for every formula $F$ in first order predicate logic and every
natural number $n$, allows one to decide if there is a proof of $F$ of
length $n$ ($\text{length} = \text{number of symbols}$).
Let $\Psi (F,n)$ be the number of steps the machine requires for this
and let $\phi (n) = \max_F \Psi(F,n)$.
The question is how fast $\phi (n)$ grows for an optimal machine.
One can show that $\phi (n) \geq k \cdot n$.
If there really were a machine with
$\phi(n) \sim k \cdot n$
(or even $\sim k \cdot n^2$),
this would have consequences of the greatest importance.
Namely, it would obviously mean that in spite of the undecidability of
the \textit{Entscheidungsproblem}, the mental work of a mathematician
concerning Yes-or-No questions could be completely replaced by a machine.
After all, one would simply have to choose the natural number $n$ so large
that when the machine does not deliver a result,
it makes no sense to think more about the problem.
Now it seems to me, however, to be
\citetext{completely within the realm of possibility
  that $\phi (n)$ grows that slowly}{%
  Hartmanis observes in \autocite{Hartmanis} that it is interesting
  that G\"odel, the person who destroyed Hilbert's program of
  automating mathematics, seemed to think that these problems quite
  possibly are solvable in linear or quadratic time.}.
Since it seems that $\phi (n) \geq k \cdot n$ is the only estimation which
one can obtain by a generalization of the proof of the undecidability
of the \textit{Entscheidungsproblem} and after all
$\phi(n) \sim k \cdot n$ (or $\sim k \cdot n^2$)
only means that the number of steps as opposed to trial and error can be
reduced from $N$ to $\log N$ (or $(\log N)^2$).
% However, such strong reductions appear in other finite problems,
% for example in the computation of the quadratic residue symbol using repeated
% application of the law of reciprocity.
\ldots{}
It would be interesting to know, for instance,
the situation concerning the determination of primality of a number and
how strongly in general the number of steps in finite combinatorial problems
can be reduced with respect to simple exhaustive search.
\end{quotation}

\noindent
So we can compare $\P$~versus~$\NP$ with the \HP.
The \HP{} and related results tell us, in the light of
Church's Thesis, what is knowable in principle. 
The $\P$~versus~$\NP$ question, in contrast,  
speaks to what we can ever, in practice, know.

\subsection{Discussion} % https://www.scottaaronson.com/papers/pnp.pdf and https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.1791.pdf
The $\P$ versus~$\NP$ question 
is certainly the sexiest one in Theoretical Computer Science today.
It has attracted a great deal of speculation, and gossip.
\citetext{In 2012 a poll}{%
  W~Gasarch is a prominent researcher and blogger in Computational
  Complexity.
  For the description see
  \url{https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/papers/poll2012.pdf}.
}
of experts found that out of 152 respondents, $83\%$ thought that
$\P\neq\NP$ while only $9\%$ thought that $\P=\NP$.\footnote{%
  There were a few other responses such as ``Don't Know''.}
This subsection discusses some of the intuition around the question. 

\margingraphic*{0.25\textwidth}{complexity/pix/jigsaw.png} % By Psyon (Own work) [CC BY-SA 3.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/b
First the intuition that $\P\neq\NP$.
When we work on a jigsaw puzzle we perceive that
checking that the solution is correct is very much easier than
finding the solution in the first place.
The checking is mechanical, tedious.
But the finding, we think, is creative\Dash solving
a jigsaw puzzle by brute-force trying every possible piece against every
other strikes us
as far too much computation to be practical.

Similarly, encryption schemes are engineered so that, given an
encrypted message, decrypting it with the key is fast and easy
but trying to brute force decrypt it by doing all possible keys is,
we perceive, just not tractable.

This is the distinction between problems in~$\P$
and problems that are $\NP$~complete.
A problem is in $\P$ if finding a solution is fast,
and a problem is in $\NP$ if verifying the correctness of a given
witness~$\omega$ is fast.
From this point of view, the result that $\P\subseteq\NP$
becomes the observation that if a problem is
fast to solve then it must be fast to verify.
But inclusion in the other direction seems to most experts to be unlikely.

Thus, informally. the $\P$ versus~$\NP$ question asks if 
finding a solution as fast as recognizing one.
If $\P=\NP$ then the two jobs are, essentially, equally difficult.
But to most people,
checking a proposed solution in polynomial time seems easier than
finding a solution in polynomial time.
These people guess that $\P\neq\NP$.

% The collective failure by many talented people to find efficient algorithms for specific NP-complete problems strongly suggests that no efficient algorithm exists for any of them.

Many commentators,
\citetext{including Cook}{See \autocite{Cook}.},
have extended this thinking outside
the bounds of Theoretical Computer Science.
``Similar remarks apply
to diverse creative human endeavors,
such as designing airplane wings, creating
physical theories, or even composing music.
The question in each case is to what
extent an efficient algorithm for recognizing a good result can be found.''
Perhaps it is hyperbole to say that if $\P=\NP$ then writing
great symphonies would
be a job for computers, a job for mechanisms,
but it is correct to say that if $\P=\NP$ and if we can write
fast algorithms to recognize excellent music\Dash and our everyday
experience with Artificial Intelligence makes this seem more and
more likely\Dash 
then we could have fast mechanical writers of excellent music.

We finish with a taste of the contrarian view.

E~Viola \autocite{Viola} observes that there are many cases where
everyone ``knew'' that some algorithm was the fastest to solve
a certain problem but it proved not to be so.
The section on Big-O begins with one, the grade school algorithm for
multiplication.
Another is the problem of solving systems
of linear equations.
The Gauss's Method algorithm is
obvious, and natural, and runs in time $\bigOh(n^3)$.\footnote{%
  Here is a small analogy for both the grade school multiplication and
  Gauss's Method results:
  consider the algebra problem of computing $ac+ad+bc+bd$.
  Someone might claim that to evaluate it ``obviously'' requires
  four multiplications.
  But then someone else counters that it equals $(a+b)(c+d)$,
  and so only requires one multiplication.
  The two cited results
  are a few among many examples evincing that naturalness and obviousness
  do not guarantee that the answer is correct, that no one will
  come along with a clever way that does the job with less.}
But while  trying to prove that Gauss's Method is optimal,
Strassen instead found
a method that is $\bigOh(n^{\lg 7})$ ($\lg 7\approx 2.81)$.
Every day  on the Theory of Computing blog feed
\autocite{blog:TOC} there are examples of this, of researchers producing
algorithms faster than the ones previously known.
R~J~Lipton expresses the same sense~\autocite{Lipton}.
\begin{quotation}
Since we are constantly discovering new ways to program our ``machines'',
why not a discovery that shows how to factor? or how to solve $\SAT$?
Why are we all so sure that there are no great new programming methods
still to be discovered?
\ldots{}
% Methods that could allow us to \ldots{} solve $\SAT$?
I am puzzled that so many are convinced that these problems could not
fall to new programming tricks, yet that is what is done each and every
day in their own research.
\end{quotation}
% Viola says, ``The evidence is clear: we have grossly
% underestimated the reach of efficient computation, in a variety of contexts.''

Knuth has a related but somewhat different take on it \autocite{Knuth}.
\begin{quotation}
Some of my reasoning is admittedly naive: It's hard to believe that
$\P\neq\NP$ and that so many brilliant people have failed to discover why.
On the other hand if you imagine a number $M$ that's finite but incredibly
large \ldots{} then there's a humongous number of possible algorithms that
do $n^M$ bitwise or addition or shift operations on $n$ given bits,
and it's really hard to believe that all of those algorithms fail.

My main point, however, is that I don't believe that the equality
$\P=\NP$ will turn out to be helpful even if it is proved,
because such a proof will almost surely be nonconstructive.
Although I think $M$ probably exists, I also think human beings will never
know such a value.
I even suspect that nobody will even know an upper bound on $M$.

Mathematics is full of examples where something is proved to exist,
yet the proof tells us nothing about how to find it.
Knowledge of the mere existence of an algorithm is completely different
from the knowledge of an actual algorithm.
\end{quotation}

Of course, all this is speculation.
Speculating is fun but in the end these researchers, and all of us,
look to settle the question with proof.

\section{Other classes}
There are many other classes, but we will see one more that delineates,
in a sense, our everyday concerns.


\subsection{Time Complexity}
The impediment a programmer runs across first is time.

\begin{definition}
Let $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$.
A decition problem for a language is an element of \definend{$\DTIME(t)$}
if it is decided by a deterministic
Turing machine that runs in time~$\bigOh(t)$.
A problem is an element of \definend{$\NTIME(t)$}
if it is decided by a nondeterministic
Turing machine that runs in time~$\bigOh(t)$. 
\end{definition}

This does not say that an algorithm is in that class,
rather it says that a language is in that class.
A language is in that class if there is an algorithm for its decision problem
that has the stated big-O runtime behavior.

\begin{lemma}
A problem is polytime, $\compclass{P}$,
if it is a member of $\DTIME(n^c)$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{equation*}
  \P =\bigcup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(n^c)          
     =\DTIME(n)\cup\DTIME(n^2)\cup\DTIME(n^3)\cup\,\cdots
\end{equation*}
The matching statements hold for 
$\compclass{NP}$ and $\EXP$.
\begin{align*}
  \NP &=\bigcup_{c\in\N}\NTIME(n^c)          
     =\NTIME(n)\cup\NTIME(n^2)\cup\NTIME(n^3)\cup\,\cdots    \\
  \EXP &=\bigcup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(2^{n^c})           
       =\DTIME(2^n)\cup\DTIME(2^{n^2})\cup\DTIME(2^{n^3})\cup\,\cdots
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The only equality that is not immediate is the last one.
Recall that a problem is in $\EXP$ if an
algorithm for it that runs in time $\bigOh(b^{p(n)})$ for some
constant base~$b$ and polynomial~$p$.
The equality above only uses the base~$2$.
To cover the discrepancy, we will show that
$3^n\in\bigOh(2^{(n^2)})$.
Consider $\lim_{x\to\infty}2^{(x^2)}/3^x$.
Rewrite the fraction as $(2^x/3)^x$, which when $x>2$ is larger than
$(4/3)^x$, which goes to infinity. 
This argument works for any base, not just $b=3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
While the above description of $\NP$ reiterates its naturalness,
the characterization that proves to be most useful in practice
is that a problem~$\prob$ is in $\NP$ if there is a deterministic
Turing machine such that for each input~$\sigma$
there is a polynomial length witness~$\omega$ and the verification
on that machine for~$\sigma$ using~$\omega$ takes polytime.
\end{remark}





\subsection{Space Complexity}
We can consider how much space
is used in solving a problem.

\begin{definition}
A deterministic Turing machine
\definend{runs in space~$\map{s}{\kleenestar{\B}}{\R^+}$}
if for all but finitely many inputs~$\sigma$, the computation on that
input takes less than or equal to $s(|\sigma|)$-many cells on the work tape.
A nondeterministic Turing machine
\definend{runs in space~$s$}
if for all but finitely many inputs~$\sigma$, every computation path on that
input takes less than or equal to $t(|\sigma|)$-many steps.
\end{definition}

The machine must use less than or equal to $s(|\sigma|)$-many
cells even on non-accepting computations.

\begin{definition}
Let $\map{s}{\N}{\N}$.
A decition problem for a language is an element of \definend{$\DSPACE(s)$}
(or \definend{$\SPACE(s)$})
if that languages is decided by a deterministic
Turing machine that runs in space~$\bigOh(s)$.
A problem is an element of \definend{$\NSPACE(s)$}
if the languages is decided by a nondeterministic
Turing machine that runs in space~$\bigOh(s)$. 
\end{definition}

The definitions arise from a sense we have of a symmetry between time and
space, that they are both examples of computational resources.
(There are other resources; for instance we may want to minimize
disk reading or writing, which may be quite different than space usage.)
But space is not just like time.
For one thing, while a program can take a long time but
use only a little space, the opposite is not possible.

\begin{lemma}
Let $\map{f}{\N}{\N}$.
Then 
$\DTIME(f)\subseteq\DSPACE(f)$
and
$\NTIME(f)\subseteq\NSPACE(f)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A machine can use at most one cell per step.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
\begin{align*}
  \PSPACE &= \bigcup_{c\in\N}\DSPACE(n^c)          
     =\DSPACE(n)\cup\DSPACE(n^2)\cup\DSPACE(n^3)\cup\,\cdots \\
  \NPSPACE &=\bigcup_{c\in\N}\NSPACE(n^c)          
     =\NSPACE(n)\cup\NSPACE(n^2)\cup\NSPACE(n^3)\cup\,\cdots
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

So $\PSPACE$ is the class of
problems that can be solved by a deterministic Turing machine using
only a polynomially-bounded amount of space,
regardless of how long the computation takes.

As even those preliminary results suggest,
restricting by space instead of time allows for a lot more power.

\begin{lemma}
$\P\subseteq\NP\subseteq\PSPACE $
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For any problem in $\NP$, check all possible witness strings~$\omega$.
These take at most polynomial space.
If any proof string works then the answer to the problem is `yes'.
Otherwise, the answer is `no'.
\end{proof}

Note that the method in the proof
may take exponential time, but it takes only polynomial space.

We finish with a result whose proof is beyond our scope, but that
serves as a caution that time and space appear very different.
We don't know whether deterministic polynomial time equals
nondeterministic polynomial time, but we do know the answer for space.

\begin{theorem}[Savitch's Theorem]
$\PSPACE=\NPSPACE$
\end{theorem}


\subsection{The Zoo}
Researchers have studied a great many complexity classes.
There are so many that they have been gathered into
an online \href{https://complexityzoo.uwaterloo.ca/Complexity_Zoo}{Complexity Zoo}.

One way to understand what is happening with these classes is that
discovering a class is a way to ask a type of Theory of Computing question.
For instance, we have already discussed that
asking whether $\NP$ equals~$\P$ is a way of asking whether
unbounded parallelism makes any essential difference\Dash can a problem
change from intractable to tractable if we switch from a deterministic
to a nondeterministic machine?
Similarly, we know that $\P\subseteq\PSPACE$.
In thinking about whether the two are equal researchers are considering
the space-time tradeoff: if you can solve a problem without much memory
does that mean you can solve it without using much time?

Here is one extra, to give some flavor of the possibilities.
For many more, see the Zoo.

The class \definend{$\compclass{BPP}$, Bounded-Error Probabilistic
Polynomial-Time} contains the 
problems solvable by an nondeterministic polytime machine such that
if the answer is `yes' then at least two-thirds of the computation paths accept
and if the answer is `no' then at most one-third of the computation paths
accept.
(Here all computation paths have the same length.)
This is often identified as the class of feasible problems for a
computer with access to a genuine random-number source.
Investigating whether $\compclass{BPP}$ equals $\P$ is asking whether
whether every efficient randomized algorithm be made deterministic:~are
there some problems for which there are fast randomized algorithms
but no fast deterministic ones?

On reading in the Zoo, a person is struck by two things.
There are many, many results listed\Dash we know a lot.
But there also are many questions
to be answered\Dash breakthroughs are there waiting for a
discoverer.

\extra{RSA Encryption}  \label{extra:RSA}

One of the great things about the interwebs,
besides that you can get free Theory of Computing books,
is that can buy stuff.
You send a credit card number and a couple of days later
the stuff appears.

For this to be practical your credit card number must be kept secret.
It must be encrypted.

When you visit a web site using a \url{https}
address, that site sends you information, called a key, that your browser
uses to encrypt your card number.
The web site then uses a different key to decrypt.
This is an important point:~the decrypter must differ from
than the encrypter since
anyone on the net can see the encrypter information that the site sent you.
But the site keeps the decrypter information private.
These two, encrypter and decrypter, form a matched pair.
We will describe the mathematical technologies that make this work.



\subsection{The arithmetic}
We can view that everything on a modern computer is numbers.
Consider the message `send money'. 
Its ASCII encoding is \str{115} \str{101} \str{110} \str{100} \str{32}
\str{109} \str{111} \str{110} \str{101} \str{121}.
Converting to a bit string gives
\str{01110011}\, \str{01100101}\, \str{01101110}\, \str{01100100}\,
\str{00100000}\, \str{01101101}\, \str{01101111}\, \str{01101110}\,
\str{01100101}\, \str{01111001}.
In decimal that's
% $544943221199950100456825$
$544\,943\,221\,199\,950\,100\,456\,825$.
So there is no loss in generality in
viewing everything we do, including encryption systems, as numerical operations.

To make such systems, mathematicians and computer scientists have
leveraged that there are
there are things we can do easily, but that we do not know how to easily
undo\Dash
that are numerical operations we can use for encryption that are fast,
but such that the operations needed to decrypt
(without the decrypter)
are believed to be so slow that they are completely impractical.
So this is engineering Big-O.

We will describe an algorithm based on the Factoring Problem.
We have algorithms for multiplying numbers that are fast.
The algorithms that
we have for starting with a number and decomposing it into factors
are, by comparison, quite slow.
To illustrate this, you might contrast the time it takes you to multiply
two four-digit numbers by hand with the time it takes you to factor an
eight-digit number chosen at random.
Set aside an afternoon for that second job, it'll take a while.

\margingraphic{0.30\textwidth}{complexity/pix/rsa.png}{\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shamir}{Adi Shamir (b~1952)}, \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Rivest}{Ron Rivest (b~1947)}, \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Adleman}{Leonard Adleman (b~1945)}} % source unknown; believed OK because very widely available

Finally they thought to use

Adleman was unable to break it since, he said,

The basis of RSA is to find three numbers, a \definend{modulus}~$n$,
an \definend{encrypter}~$e$, and a \definend{decrypter}~$d$,
related by this equation
(here $m$ is the message, as a number).
\begin{equation*}
(m^{e})^{d}\equiv m \pmod {n} 
\end{equation*}
The encrypted message is $m^e\bmod{n}$.
To decrypt it, to recover~$m$, calculate $(m^e)^d\bmod{n}$.
These three are chosen so that
knowing $e$ and~$n$, or even~$m$, still leaves a potential secret-cracker 
who is looking for~$d$ with an extremely difficult job.

To choose them,
first choose distinct prime numbers $p$ and~$q$.
Pick these at random so they are of about equal bit-lengths.
Compute $n=pq$ and $\varphi(n)=(p-1)\cdot(q-1)$.
Next, choose~$e$ with $1<e<\varphi(n)$ that is realtively prime to~$n$.
Finally, find $d$ as the multiplicative inverse of~$e$ modulo~$n$.
(We shall show below that all these operations, including using the
keys for encryption and decryption, can be done quickly.)

The pair $\sequence{n,e}$ is the \definend{public key}
and the pair $\sequence{n,d}$ is the \definend{private key}.
The length of $d$ in bits is the \definend{key length}.
Most experts consider a key length of $2\,048$ bits to be
secure for the mid-term future, until 2030 or so,
when computers will have grown in power enough that they may be able
to use an exhaustive brute-force search to find~$d$.

\begin{example}
Alice chooses the primes $p=101$ and~$q=113$
(these are too small to use in practice but are good for an
illustration) and then calculates
$n=pq=11\,413$ and $\varphi(n)=(p-1)(q-1)=11\,200$.
To get the encrypter she randomly picks numbers $1<e<11\,200$ until she
gets one that is
relatively prime to $11\,200$, choosing $e=3533$.
She publishes her public key $\sequence{n,e}=\sequence{11\,413, 3\,533}$
on her home page.
She computes the decrypter $d=e^{-1}\bmod{11\,200}=6\,597$, and
finds a safe place to store her private key
$\sequence{n,d}=\sequence{11\,413,6\,597}$.

Bob wants to say `Hi'.
In ASCII that's \str{01001000} \str{01101001}.
If he converted that string into a single decimal number it would be bigger
than~$n$ so he breaks it into
two substrings, getting the decimals \str{72} and \str{105}.
Using her public key he computes
\begin{equation*}
  72^{3533}\bmod{11413}=10496
  \qquad
  105^{35333}\bmod{11413}=4861
\end{equation*}
and sends Alice the sequence $\sequence{10496,4861}$.

\begin{equation*}
  10496^{6597}\bmod{11413} = 72
  \qquad
  4861^{6597}\bmod{11413} = 105
\end{equation*}
\end{example}

\subsection{The arithmetic, fast}
We've just illustrated that RSA uses invertible operations.
There are lots of ways to get invertible operations
so our understanding of RSA is incomplete unless we know why it uses these
particular operations.
As discussed above, the important point is that they can be done quickly,
but undoing them, finding the decrypter, is believed to take a very
long time.

We start with a classic, beautiful, result from Number Theory.

\begin{theorem}[Prime Number Theorem]
The number of primes less than $n\in\N$ is approximately
$n/\ln(n)$; that is, this limit is~$1$.
\begin{equation*} 
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\text{number of primes less than $x$}}{(x/\ln x)}
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
This shows the number of primes less than~$n$ for some values up to a million.
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/xygraphs/xygraphs00.pdf}
\end{center}
This theorem says that primes are common.
For example, the number of primes less than $2^{1024}$ is about
$2^{1024}/\ln(2^{1024})
  \approx 2^{1014}/709.78
  \approx 2^{1024}/2^{9.47}
  \approx 2^{1015}$.
% sage: 1024*ln(2)
% 1024*log(2)
% sage: 1024*ln(2).n()
% 709.782712893384
% sage: ln(1024*ln(2))/ln(2)
% log(1024*log(2))/log(2)
% sage: (ln(1024*ln(2))/ln(2)).n()
% 9.47123362705510
% sage: 1024-(ln(1024*ln(2))/ln(2)).n()
% 1014.52876637294
% sage: 1/(1024*ln(2))  
Said another way, if we choose a number~$n$ at random then the probability
that it is prime is about $1/\ln(n)$ and so 
a random number that is $1024$ bits long will be a prime with probability
about $1/(\ln(2^{1024}))\approx 1/710$.
% sage: 1/(1/(1024*ln(2)).n())
% 709.782712893384
On average
we need only select $355$ odd numbers of about that size 
before we find a prime.
Hence we can efficiently generate large primes by just picking random numbers,
as long as we can efficiently test their primality.

On our way to giving an efficient way to test primality,
we observe that the operations of multiplication and addition
modulo~$m$ are efficient.
(We will give examples only, rather than the full analysis
of the operations.)

\begin{example}
Multiplying $3\,915\,421$ by $52\,567\,004$
modulo~$3$ looks hard.
The naive approach is to first take their product and then 
divide by~$3$ to find the remainder.
But there is a more efficient way.
Rather than multiply first and then reduce modulo~$m$, reduce first
and then multiply.
That is, we know that if $a\equiv b\pmod{m}$ and 
$c\equiv d\pmod{m}$ then $ac\equiv bd\pmod{m}$ and so
since $3\,915\,421\equiv 1\pmod{3}$
and $52\,567\,004\equiv 2\pmod{3}$ we have this.
\begin{equation*}
  3\,915\,421\cdot 52\,567\,004 
  \equiv 1\cdot 2\pmod{3} 
\end{equation*}
% In this way, multiplication modulo~$m$ is efficient in both time and space.
\end{example}

Similarly, exponentiation modulo $m$ is also efficient,
both in time and in space.

\begin{example}
Consider raising $4$ to the $13$-th power, modulo~$m=497$.
The naive approach would be to raise $4$ to the $13$-th power,
which is a very large number, and reduce
modulo~$497$.  
But there is a better way. 

Start by expressing the power~$13$ in base~$2$ as $13=8+4+1=1101_2$.
So, $4^{13}=4^{8}\cdot 4^4\cdot 4^1$ and we need the $8$-th power,
the $4$-th power, and the first power of~$4$.
If we can efficiently get those powers
then we can multiply them modulo~$m$ efficiently, so
we will be set.

Get the powers by repeated squaring (modulo~$m$).
Start with $p=1$.
Squaring gives $4^2$, then squaring again gives $4^4$, and
squaring again gives $4^8$.
Getting these powers (modulo~$m$) just requires a multiplication,
which we can do efficiently.
\end{example}

The last thing we need for efficiently testing primality
is to efficiently find the multiplicative inverse modulo~$m$.
Recall that two numbers are
\definend{relatively prime} or \definend{coprime}
if their greatest common divisor is~$1$.
For example, $15=3\cdot 5$ and $22=2\cdot 11$ are relatively prime.

\begin{lemma}
If $a$ and~$m$ are relatively prime then there is an inverse for~$a$ 
modulo~$m$, a number~$k$ such that $a\cdot k\equiv 1\pmod{m}$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Because the greatest common divisor of $a$ and~$m$ is~$1$, Euclid's algorithm 
gives a linear combination of the two, a $sa+tm$ for some $s,t\in\Z$, 
that adds to~$1$.
Doing the operations modulo~$m$ gives $sa+tm\equiv 1\pmod{m}$.
Since $tm$ is a multiple of~$m$, we have $tm\equiv 0\pmod m$, leaving 
$sa\equiv 1\pmod m$, and
$s$ is the inverse of~$a$ modulo~$m$.
\end{proof}

Euclid's algorithm is efficient, both in time and space,
so finding an inverse modulo~$m$ is efficient.

Now we can test for primes.
The simplest way to test whether a number~$n$ is prime is to try  
dividing~$n$ by all possible factors.
But that is very slow.
There is a faster way, based on the next result.

\begin{theorem}[Fermat] For a prime~$p$, if $a\in\Z$
is not divisible by~$p$ then $a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $a$ be an integer not divisible by the prime~$p$.
Multiply~$a$ by each number $i\in\set{1,\ldots\,p-1}$ and
reduce modulo~$p$ to get the numbers $r_i=ia\bmod{p}$.

We will show that the set $R=\set{r_1,\ldots\,r_{p-1}}$
equals the set $P=\set{1,\ldots\,p-1}$.
First, $R\subseteq P$.
Because $p$ is prime and does not divide $i$ or~$a$, it does not divide their
product~$ia$.
Thus $r_i=ia\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}$ and so all the $r_i$ are members of the
set $\set{1,\ldots\,p-1}$.

To get inclusion the other way, $P\subseteq R$, note that
if $i_0\neq i_1$ then $r_{i_0}\neq r_{i_1}$.
For, with $r_0-r_1=i_0a-i_1a=(i_0-i_1)a$, because $p$ is prime and
does not divide $i_0-i_1$ or~$a$ (as each is smaller in absolute value
than~$p$), it does not divide their product.
That means that the two sets have the same number of elements, so
$P\subseteq R$.

Now multiply together all of the elements of that set.
\begin{align*}
  a\cdot 2a\cdots (p-1) a
    &\equiv 1\cdot 2\cdots\,(p-1)\pmod{p} \\
  (p-1)!\cdot a^{p-1}
    &\equiv (p-1)!\pmod{p} \\
\end{align*}
Cancelling the $(p-1)!$'s gives the result.
\end{proof}

Given~$n$, if we find a base~$a$ with~$0<a<n$ so that 
$a^{n-1}\bmod{n}$ is not~$1$ then $n$ is not prime.

\begin{example}
Consider $n=415\,692$.
If $a=2$ then
$2^{415692}\equiv 58346\pmod{415693}$
so $n$ is not prime.
\end{example}

There are $n$'s where $a^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$ but $n$ is not prime.
Such a number is a \definend{Fermat liar} or \definend{Fermat pseudoprime}
with base~$a$.
One for base $a=2$ is $n = 341=11\cdot 31$.
However, \citetext{computer searches suggest that these are very rare}{%
  For instance, among the numbers less than $2.5\times 10^{10}$ 
  there are only $21\,853\approx 2.2\times 10^{4}$ pseudoprimes base $2$;
  that's six orders of magnitude.}.

The rarity of exceptions suggests that we use a
\definend{probabilistic primality test}:~given $n\in\N$ to test for
primality,
pick at random a base~$a$ with $0<a<n$ and calculate whether
$a^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
If that is not true then $n$ is not prime.\footnote{%
  In this case $a$ is a witness to
  the fact that~$n$ is not prime.}
If it is true then we have evidence that~$n$ is prime.

Researchers have shown that if $n$ is not prime then
each choice of base~$a$ has a greater than $1/2$ chance of finding that
$a^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
So if $n$ were not prime and we did the test with two different bases
$a_0,a_1$ then
there would be a less than $(1/2)^2$ chance of getting both
$a_0^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$ and $a_1^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
So we figure that there is at least a $1-(1/2)^2$ chance that $n$ is prime. 
After $k$-many iterations of choosing a base, doing the
calculation, and never finding that that $n$ is not prime, then
we have a greater than $1-(1/2)^k$ chance that~$n$ is prime.

In summary,
if~$n$ passes $k$-many tests for \citetext{any reasonable-sized~$k$}{%
  Selecting an appropriate $k$ is an engineering choice between the cost
  of extra iterations and the gain in confidence.}
  then \citetext{we are quite confident that it is prime}{%
  We are confident, but not sure.
  There are numbers, called \definend{Carmichael numbers},
  that are pseudoprime for every base~$a$ relatively prime to~$n$.
  The smallest example is $n = 561 = 3\cdot 11\cdot17$,
  and the next two are $1\,105$ and~$1\,729$.
  Like pseudoprimes, these seem to be very rare.
  Among the numbers less than $10^{16}$ there are about
  $2.7\times 10^{14}$ primes but only
  $246\ 683 \approx 2.4\times 10^5$-many Carmichael numbers.}.
Our interest in this
test is that it is extremely fast; it runs in time
$\bigOh (k \cdot (\log n)^2 \cdot \log \log n \cdot \log \log \log n)$.
So we can run it lots of times, becoming very confident, in not
very much time.

\begin{example}
We could test whether $n=7$ is prime by computing, say,  
that $3^{6}\equiv 1\pmod{7}$,
and $5^{6}\equiv 1\pmod{7}$, and $6^{6}\equiv 1\pmod{7}$.
The fact that $n=7$ does not fail makes us confident it is prime. 
\end{example}

The RSA algorithm also uses this offshoot of Fermat's Little Theorem.

\begin{corollary}
Let $p$ and~$q$ be unequal primes and
suppose that $a$ is not divisible by either one.
Then $a^{(p-1)(q-1)}\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Fermat, $a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p}$ and $a^{q-1}\equiv 1\pmod{q}$.
Raise the first to the $q-1$ power and the second to the $p-1$ power.
\begin{equation*}
  a^{(p-1)(q-1)}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
  \qquad
  a^{(p-1)(q-1)}\equiv 1\pmod{q}
\end{equation*}
Since  $a^{(p-1)(q-1)}-1$ is divisible by both $p$ and~$q$,
it is divisible by their product $pq=n$.
\end{proof}

Experts think that the most likely attack on RSA encryption is by
factoring the modulus~$n$.
Anyone who factors~$n$ can use the same method as the
RSA key setup process to turn the encrypter~$e$
into the decrypter~$d$.
That's why~$n$ is taken to be the product of two large primes; it makes
factoring as hard as possible.

There is a factoring algorithm that takes only $\bigOh(b^3)$~time
(and $\bigOh((b)$~space), called
Shor's algorithm. 
But it runs only on quantum computers.
No one has to this point built such a machine and 
whether such machines will ever become practical is not clear.
For instance, in 2001 the first seven-qubit quantum computer
became the first to run Shor's algorithm.
It factored the number $15$.
There has been progress in the interim, but not enough to put RSA at risk.

\begin{exercise}
To test whether a number~$n$ is prime, we could just try dividing it

\item

\item
For input~$n=10^{12}$ how many numbers would you need to test?
\item
Show that this is a terrible algorithm since it is exponential in the

\end{exparts}
\begin{answer}
\begin{exparts}
\item
With two factors $n=k_0\cdot k_1$ if one is greater than $\sqrt{n}$ then

\item

\item

\item
The input~$n$ has size about $\lg(n)$~bits.
Thus $\sqrt{n}\approx\sqrt{2^{\text{size of $n$}}}
  \approx ^{(1/2)\cdot\text{size of $n$}}$.
\end{exparts}
\end{answer}

\extra{Tractability and good-enoughness}
Are we taking the right approach to characterizing the behavior of algorithms,
to understanding the complexity of comptuations?

A theory, any theory, shapes the way that you look at the world.
For instance, when Newton's $F=ma$ points
to an approach to analyzing physical situations: if you see a change,
look for a force.
Of course, that approach has been fantastically successful, enabling us
to build bridges, send people to the moon, etc.

So we should ask if our theory is right.
Of course, the theorems are right\Dash the proofs check out, the
results stand up to formalization, etc.
But it is healthy to examine the current approach
to ask whether there is a better way to
see the problems in front of us.

In the theory we've outlined,
Cobham's Thesis identifies $\P$ with the tractable problems.
However, the situation as we know it today is not so neat.

First, there are some problems known to be in $\P$, for which we do not
know a practical apporach.
For one thing, as we discussed when we introduced Cobham's Thesis,
a problem for which the smallest possible algorithm is $\bigOh(n^{1000})$
is not practical.
Neither is one whose algorithm is $\bigOh(n^2)$ but whose running time
on closer inspection proves to be $2^{1000}n^2$.

There are many results that give answers that are practical for
problems that our theory suggests are intractible.
And many problems that are attackable in theory turn out to be
awkward.
We will give one example of an extension of the theory we've outlined
that shows promise.
It is called \definend{Parametrized Complexity}.

We summarize with a quote.  % Parametrized Complexity, Downey and Fellows
\begin{quotation}
It is worthwhile keeping in mind that intractability must be coped with
and problems must be solved in practical applications.
Furthermore, it is important to keep in sight the fact that in applications,
we are only dealing with a finite fragment of the
``asymptotic universe,'' so asymptotic classes such as $\P$ only
approximate practical feasibility in the same way that classes
like $\NP$ and $\compclass{PSPACE}$ approximate
practical intractability.
From an engineering point of view, there are certainly approaches to
complexity other than the classical view which can yield
significant insight and applications for practical problems.
\end{quotation}

The idea of this approach is that
we do not need to be uniformily efficient on inputs of all sizes, only
on those that are in some sense small.

Instead of expressing the running time as a function $T(n)$ of the input size
$n$, we express it as a function
$T(n, k)$ of the input size and some parameter $k$ of the input.
Examples of such parameters are:
the maximum degree of the input graph,
the diameter of the input graph, or
the length of clauses in the input Boolean formula.
