In the first part of the  book we studies which problems are solvable on a
computer.
Computational complexity tries to determine which problem are
\emph{efficiently} solvable on a computer.

We will review the basics of the orders of growth of functions.
Because this is a review, we will not give a detailed development, just enough for our needs.
And we will leave the proofs for the exercises.

\section{Big O}
For a problem there may be many algorithms.
We typically want to choose one that is faster or uses less memory,
or less of some other resource.
The rate at which algorithms use resources 
is a function of the size of their input.
We have tools to describe the rates.

First, an anecdote.
Here is the grade school multiplication algorithm.
% How long does it take?
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{array}{@{}r@{}}
    678\\
    \times 42\\  \hline
    1356\\
   +27120\\  \hline
   28476
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
For each digit of the multiplier~$42$ we 
multiply each digit of the multiplicand~$678$.
That's a nested loop.
The natural guess is that multiplication of two $n$-bit numbers
requires, at worst, a time of about $n^2$ ticks.

% https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Andrej_Nikolajewitsch_Kolmogorov.jpg
\margingraphic{0.20\textwidth}{complexity/Kolmogorov.jpg}{\label{fig:kolmogorov}\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov}{Andrey Kolmogorov 1903--1987}}
In 1960 A~Kolmogorov organized a seminar at Moscow State University with the
goal of proving this.
Within a week one of his students,
\citetext{A~Karatsuba}{See \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Karatsuba}.},
had found that it is false.
Karatsuba produced a 
\citetext{clever algorithm}{%
The idea is to let $k = \ceiling{n/2}$ 
and write $x = x_1 2^k + x_0$ and $y = y_1 2^k + y_0$
(so $678=21 \cdot 2^5+6$ and $42=1\cdot 2^5+10$). 
Then we have $xy = A\cdot 2^{2k} + B\cdot 2^k + C$ 
where $A = x_1 y_1$, and $B = x_1 y_0 + x_0 y_1$, and~$C = x_0 y_0$
(here, $28\,476=21\cdot 2^{10}+216\cdot 2^5+60$). 
The multiplications by $2^{2k}$ and $2^{k}$ are just bit-shifts to known 
locations independent of the values of $x$ and $y$, 
so they don't affect the time much. 
But the two multiplications for 
$B$ seem remove all the advantage and still give $\Theta(n^2)$ time.
However, what Karatsuba noted instead was that
$B = (x_0 + x_1)\cdot (y_0 + y_1) - A - C$
Boom: done.
Just one multiplication.}
that used about
$n^{\lg(3)}\approx n^{1.585}$ ticks.
At the next meeting 
Kolmogorov explained the result 
and closed the seminar.

This story illustrates a theme in the Theory of Computation:
perusing the Theory of Computation blog feed \autocite{blog:TOC}
shows that every day researchers
produce results saying, ``here is a job and here is a way to do it in less time,
or less space, etc.'' 
We are good at finding clever ways to do a job and thereby
reducing the upper bound
rate at which some computation resource, such as time,
is consumed while solving the problem.
But we are not good at proving nontrivial lower bounds, at saying
``no algorithm can do this job in less than this number of ticks.'' 
That's one reason why in the development below we will
mostly compare the growth rates of functions using a measure that is
about `less than or equal to'. 




\subsection{Motivation}
Suppose that we have a job to do and two algorithms with which to do it.
These have the property that when the input is size~$n\in\N$,
the first algorithm takes about $\sqrt{n}$ many ticks in the worst case,
while the second takes about $10\cdot \lg(n)$ ticks.\footnote{%
  Recall that $\lg(n)=\log_2(n)$ is defined by: start with~$n$ and then find
  the power of $2$
  that produces it.
  So, if $n=8$ then $\lg(n)=3$.
  If $n=10$ then $\lg(n)\approx 3.32$.}

For small $n$ the first algorithm's square root seems preferable to
the second's logarithm.
For instance, when the input is~$1000$
the two output values are $\sqrt{1000}\approx 31.62$
and $10 \lg(1000)\approx 99.66$.

\end{center}
However,
in the long run the values of $\sqrt{n}$ are much larger than
the values of $10\lg(n)$.
For instance, $\sqrt{1\,000\,000}=1\,000$ while
$10\lg(1\,000\,000)\approx 199.32$.

seeing which always has smaller output values.

\subsection{Definition}
We are studying functions that describe machine resource use.
These functions have natural number inputs
giving the size of the machine's inputs.
They often have natural number outputs but we 
need functions with real number outputs, such as~$\lg$.
The outputs are usually non-negative real numbers,
but a polynomial such as $n^2-5n+2$ has negative outputs for a few
small inputs and 
$\lg$ is not even defined 
for input~$0$.
So we will focus on functions 
$\map{f}{\N}{\R}$ that are \definend{eventually nonnegative}, where
there is an~$I\in\N$ such that $n>I$ implies that $f(n)$ is defined
and nonnegative.

\begin{definition}  \label{def:BigO}
  Let $g$ be a function that is eventually nonnegative.
  Then \definend{$\bigOh(g)$}, read ``big-O of $g$,''
  is the collection of eventually nonnegative functions~$f$
  satisfying this property:~there are numbers $N,C\in\N$ so that 
  whenever $n\geq N$ then $C\cdot g(n)\geq f(n)$. 
  We say that \definend{$f$ is in $\bigOh(g)$},
  that \definend{$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$},
  or that \definend{$f=\bigOh(g)$}.
\end{definition}

Think of `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' as meaning that
$f$'s growth rate is less than or equal to $g$'s rate.
These illustrate.
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo10.pdf}
  \hspace{3em}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/bigo/bigo11.pdf}  
\end{center}
The sketch on the left has $g$ accelerating away
from~$f$ so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ since
its rate is smaller.
The sketch on the right is that $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ because
the rates are alike.

\begin{remark}
  \begin{parenum}
    \item
      We use the letter `O' because this is about the order of growth.
      % although the $\bigOh$~symbol only expresses an
      % upper bound.
    \item
      The `$f=\bigOh(g)$' notation is awkward but 
      standard.\footnotemark{} %
      The notation `$f\in\bigOh(g)$' is correct but not common.
    \item
      Some authors allow negative real outputs and write the
      inequality with absolute values, 
      $f(x)\leq C\cdot |g(x)|$.
    \item
      You often see constructions such as `$n^2+5n+6$ is $\bigOh(n^2)$',
      replacing the named functions $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=n^2$
      with expressions.
    \item
      Sometimes you see `$f$ is $\bigOh(g)$' stated as
      `$f(n)$ is $\bigOh(g(n))$'.
      Strictly speaking, this is
      wrong\Dash $f(n)$ and $g(n)$ are numbers, not functions, and big-O
      is a relationship between two functions.
  \end{parenum}
\end{remark}\footnotetext{%
  One way that it is awkward is that it does not follow the usual rules
  of equality.
  For one thing, you cannot rewrite it as `$\protect\bigOh(g)=f$'.
  Another way is that $n=\bigOh(n^2)$ and $n^2=\bigOh(n^2)$ does not
  allow us to conclude that $n=n^2$.}

\begin{example} \label{ex:BigOFromDefn}
  To directly apply the definition, given two functions we must produce
  suitable $N$ and~$C$, and verify that they work.
  Let $f(n)=n^2$ and $g(n)=n^3$.
  Then $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$, as $N=2$ and $C=1$ witness.
  The verification is that $n>2$ implies that $g(n)=n^3=n\cdot n^2$ is greater
  than $2\cdot n^2$, which is greater than $n^2=1\cdot f(n)$.

  Simiilarly, $f(n)=5n^2$ and $g(n)=n^4$ then to show $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$
  take $N=2$ and $C=2$.
  To verify:
  $n> 2$ implies that $C\cdot n^4=2\cdot n^2\cdot n^2\geq 8n^2> 5\cdot n^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The assertion that $f$ in~$\bigOh(g)$ does not mean that the graph of $g$ is
  eventually above the graph of~$f$.
  Consider $f(n)=n^2+1$ and~$g(n)=n^2$.
  The outputs of the second function
  are always less than those of the first, but $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$.
  To verify, take $N=2$ and~$C=2$.
  If $n>N$ we can write $n=j+2$ for $j\in\N^+$ and
  then $C\cdot g(n)=2n^2=2(j+2)^2=2j^2+8j+8$ is greater than
  $f(n)=(j+2)^2+1=j^2+4j+5$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  The zero function~$Z$ is $\bigOh(g)$ for every
  eventually nonnegative~$g$.
  Just take $N=0$ and~$C=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Not every pair of eventually nonnegative functions is comparable.
Consider this function in comparison with $g(n)=n^3$.
\begin{equation*}
  f(n)=\begin{cases} 
         n^2  &\case{if $n$ is even}  \\
         n^4  &\case{if $n$ is odd}
       \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
To see that $f$ is not~$\bigOh(n^3)$, observe that there are
arbitrarily large inputs~$n$ where $C=2$ does not suffice:
if $n$ is odd and $n>2$  then $f(n)=n^4=n\cdot n^3> 2\cdot n^3$.
Similar reasoning works for any~$C$.
The argument that $n^3$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$ goes the same way, on even inputs.
So the ordering imposed by $\bigOh{}$ on the set of
functions is only partial.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
We often omit the technicality of `eventually nonnegative',
as in the following.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic properties]  \label{lem:BigOAlgProps}
  Suppose that $f_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$ and that $f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_1)$.
\begin{parenum}
\item 
  For any positive constant $a\in\R^+$, the function $af_0$ is $\bigOh(g_0)$.
\item
  The sum $f_0+f_1$ is $\bigOh(g)$, where $g$ is defined by
  $g(n)=\max(g_0(n),g_1(n))$.  
  So if both $f_0$ and $f_1$ are $\bigOh(g)$ then $f_0+f_1$ is also $\bigOh(g)$.
\item
  The product $f_0f_1$ is $\bigOh(g_0g_1)$.
\end{parenum}
\end{lemma}


\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigOhReflexiveAndTransitive}
  The big-O relation is reflexive:~$f$ is $\bigOh(f)$.
  It is also transitive:~if $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and $g$ is~$\bigOh(h)$ then
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(h)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}
  Two eventually nonnegative functions have
  \definend{the same growth rate} if both $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ and also
  $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$.
  We say \definend{$f$ is $\bigTheta(g)$} or, what is the same thing,
  $g$ is~$\bigTheta(f)$).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}  \label{lem:BigThetaEquivalence}
  The relation between functions of having the same growth rate
  is an equivalence.
\end{lemma}


\begin{example}
  The order $\bigOh(n\cdot \lg(n))$ appears often.
  We will show that it equals $\bigOh(\lg(n!))$ by mutual containment of sets.
  
  Recall that logarithmic functions turn multiplication into addition:
  $\lg(a\cdot b)=\lg(a)+\lg(b)$.
  The $\bigOh(\lg(n!))\subseteq\bigOh(n\lg(n))$ direction follows from
  this.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots \lg(2) + \lg(1)            \\
            &< \lg(n) + \lg(n)   + \cdots + \lg(n)+\lg(n) = n\cdot\lg(n)
  \end{align*}
  The $\bigOh(n\lg(n))\subseteq\bigOh(\lg(n!))$ direction is
  trickier.
  \begin{align*}
    \lg(n!) &= \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(2) + \lg(1)   \\
            &> \lg(n) + \lg(n-1) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2})   \\
            &> \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \lg(\floor{n/2}) + \cdots +\lg(\floor{n/2}) \\
            &=(n/2)\cdot\lg(n/2)                      \\
            &=(n/2)\bigl[ \lg(n)-\lg(2) \bigr]
             =(1/2)\cdot n\lg(n)-(1/2)\cdot n\lg(2)
  \end{align*}
\end{example}


\begin{theorem}  \label{lem:LHBigO}
  Let $f,g$ be eventually nonegative and suppose that 
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup\set{\infty}$.
  \begin{parenum}
  \item If $L=\infty$ then $f$ grows faster than~$g$,
    meaning that $g$ is~$\bigOh(f)$ but $f$ is not $\bigOh(g)$.
  \item If $L=0$ then $g$ grows faster than~$f$,
    so $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
  \item If $L$ is strictly between $0$ and~$\infty$
  then the two functions have the same growth rate so that
    $f$ is $\bigOh(g)$ and~$g$ is $\bigOh(f)$,
    that is, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.
  \end{parenum}
\end{theorem}

Directly applying the defintion of
$\bigOh$, as in \cref{ex:BigOFromDefn}, is sometimes the best way
to go.
But often
this result from first semester calculus about the limit of
a ratio of two functions makes the job easier.

\begin{theorem}[L'H\^{o}pital's Rule]
Suppose that $\map{f,g}{\R}{\R}$ are such that as $x\to\infty$ then both
$f(x)\to\infty$ and $g(x)\to\infty$.
If 
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f^\prime(x)/g^\prime(x)$ exists and equals
$L\in\R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$
then
$\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{theorem}

Taking the derivative requires functions with inputs that are real numbers,
not natural numbers,
so we need the following observation in order to apply
L'H\^opital's Rule to \cref{lem:LHBigO}.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns}
  Let $\map{F,G}{\R}{\R}$ and let $\map{f,g}{\N}{\leftclosed{0}{\infty}}$
  be such that
  for all natural numbers $F(n)=f(n)$ and $G(n)=g(n)$.
  If $\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)/G(x)$ exists and equals $L\in\R\cup{\set{\infty}}$
  then $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ also exists and also equals~$L$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{example}
  For polynomial functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule works well.
  Consider again $\fcn{f,g}{\N}{\N}$ given by 
  $f(n)=n^2+5n+6$ and $g(n)=2n^2+3n+4$.
  On the associated real functions L'H\^{o}pital's Rule gives this. 
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2+3x+4}{x^2+5x+6}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x+3}{2x+5}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4}{2}
    =2
  \end{equation*}
  By \cref{lem:ReFcnsGiveNatFcns} the limit of the natural number functions
  $\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)$ is also~$2$.
  With that, \cref{lem:LHBigO} says that the two
  have the same growth rate, $f$ is~$\bigTheta(g)$.

  Similarly, for $f(n)=2n^2+3n+4$ and $g(n)=n^3+5n+6$,
  L'H\^opital's Rule shows that
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^3+5x+6}{2x^2+3x+4}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+5x+6}{4x+3}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{6x}{4}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  and so $f$ grows at a rate strictly slower than~$g$, that is,
  $f$ is~$\bigOh(g)$ but $g$ is not~$\bigOh(f)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  Non-polynomial functions also come up often.
  Compare $f(n)=n^2$ with $g(x)=2^n$,
  convert to real functions and apply L'H\^{o}pital's Rule.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x}{x^2}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot\ln(2)}{2x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2^x\cdot(\ln(2))^2}{2}
    =\infty
  \end{equation*}
  Thus, by \cref{lem:LHBigO},
  $f$'s order is strictly less than $g$'s\Dash $f$ is in~$\bigOh(g)$ but
  $g$ is not in~$\bigOh(f)$.

  The same approach also shows that the order of $f(x)=\lg(x)$ is 
  strictly less than the order of $g(x)=x$.
  \begin{equation*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\lg(x)}{x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)\cdot(1/\ln(2))}{1}
    =0
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}

We can convert among logarithms at the cost of a constant factor.
For instance the natural log relates to the common log (the base~$10$ log)
by $\ln(x)=\log_{10}(x)/\log_{10}(e)\approx \log_{10}(x)/0.434$.
Similarly, $\lg(x)=\log_2(x)=\ln(x)/\ln(2)\approx \ln(x)/0.693$.
In general, $\log_b(x)=\log_c(x)/\log_c(b)$.
Thus all logs have the same order of growth.
For instance,
$\ln$ is $\bigOh(\lg)$ and $\lg$ is $\bigOh(\ln)$, so that
$\ln$ is $\bigTheta(\lg)$. 

\begin{example} \label{ex:CompareLogToPower}
For any exponent~$d>0$ the power function~$x^d$
is greater, in a big-O sense, than the logarithmic function:
$\log_b(x)$ is~$\bigOh(x^d)$.
Apply L'Hopital's Rule
\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln(x)}{x^d}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)}{x^d}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x^{d+1}}
\end{equation*}
and observe that is $d>0$ then $d+1>1$ and so the limit is~$0$.
Thus, $\lg(x)$ is $\bigOh(x^{1})$
and  $\lg(x)$ is $\bigOh(x^{0.1})$
and  $\lg(x)$ is $\bigOh(x^{0.01})$, etc.
None of these comparisons reverse; \cref{exer:LogSmallerThanPower} shows that
$x^d$ is not~$\bigOh(\log_b(x))$.

The log-linear function $x\cdot\lg(x)$ has a similar relationship to the
polynomials around it.
The power function
$x^d$ for $d>1$ is greater, in a big-O sense, than $x\cdot\lg(x)$.
\begin{multline*}
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x\ln(x)}{x^d}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x\cdot (1/x)+1\cdot\ln(x)}{dx^{d-1}}
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1+\ln(x)}{dx^{d-1}}          \\
  =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1/x)}{d(d-1)x^{d-2}}
  =(1/(d)(d-1))\cdot\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x^{d-1}}
  =0
\end{multline*}
\end{example}



\subsection{Discussion}
Big-O gives a rough metric to compare algorithms and
is so widely used in practice that it is often covered in
introduction to programming courses. 
Here we will address some questions growing out of 
the experience that some people have in those classes.

% https://cs.stackexchange.com/questions/9957/justification-for-neglecting-constants-in-big-o
The first issue is that
\citetext{the definitions of Big-O and Big-Theta ignore constants}{%
  This discussion originated as \autocite{SE:IgnoreConstants}.}.
A reader can object that doing so makes the definition useless
for judging the practicality of an algorithm.
Imagine
an algorithm that takes time given by the quadratic function $c\cdot n^2$.
An enormous coefficient such as $c=2^{100}$ would make it
just as impractical as one that takes exponential time.\footnote{%
  Although, in practice, often when an algorithm is discovered that has a
  very large leading coefficient then soon researchers develop
  improvements that lower the coefficient to a usable level.
  In the same way, while in the past we have sometimes seen
  algorithms that are polynomial but with 
  large powers, such as $n^{10}$, often these powers have fallen
  as researchers refine the approach.}
(Similarly, an enormous $N$ destroys the value of the algorithm for
practical purposes.)
Why ignore the constants?

Basically, we have no unit that we can rely on.

Machines on which algorithms are implemented vary widely.
Even if we forget about such things as Turing machines and just focus on
the architecture of the machines
that we have on our desks and in our pockets, they
still vary,
for instance in how long they take to do a division or to write to memory.
We could agree on
\citetext{a standard reference machine}{%
  This is the approach of Knuth's
  \protect\textit{Art of Computer Programming} series.
  But even he had to update his standard, from MIX to MMIX.}
but we would have to periodically update the standard to keep up with
changes.
So published results from, say, a decade ago would be problematic
because they refer to an old standard.
Being reference independent is an advantage.

Consider this geographical analogy:
absolute measurement
means specifying units, such as miles or kilometers.
Being able to make statesments irrespective of the units means
making relative statements such as,
``from here New York City is twice as far as Boston.'', this
relative statement is correct irrespective of the units. 
Being reference independent precisely means ignoring constants
and instead making relative statements.

The advantage of ignoring constants is that we can do an analysis by
counting the number of elementary operations, 
We do not have to worry about costs of individual operations,
about the difference between the cost of a division and the
cost of a memory write,
as long as those costs lie between fixed upper and lower bounds.
However, the price to pay for that advantage is that
constant factors are meaningless,
since changing units is inseparable from changing constant factors\Dash
changing from miles to kilometers requires multiplying by the factor~$1.61$.

Thus, 
Big~O notation is a unit free, relative measurement.
It is not absolute.
It can only measure performance variation.
% The advantage is the gain in implementation independence
% by allowing analysis that ignores the relative costs of
% elementary operations, as long as these costs are bounded.
% But the consequence is that constant factors are meaningless.

Put another way, Big~O statements are a tool for
understanding how an algorithm
performs as the input size grows.
Thus, we say that a function that takes $3n$ steps is $\bigOh(n)$
in order to express that, roughly,
doubling the input size will no more than double the number of steps taken.
Similarly, if an algorithm is $\bigOh(n^2)$ then
doubling the input size will at most
quadruple the number of steps, and
$\bigOh(\lg n)$ means that doubling the input size will increase the number
of steps by at most some constant.
We can use Big~O to compare algorithms only in that we can compare
roughly how they perform as the input size grows.
But it is only a rough guide;
it cannot say with great precision which algorithms are absolutely
faster.\footnote{%
  Big~O is especially poor at
  for comparing performance on a specified computing platform.
  That's what benchmarks are for.}


% https://stackoverflow.com/a/19647659
That leads us to
\citetext{the second issue with the definition of Big~O and Big~Theta}{%
  This discussion originated as \autocite{SE:pseudopolynomial}.}.
Understanding how an algorithm
performs as the input size grows
requires a definition of input size.
But often in beginning programming courses that definition is blurred. 

Consider an algorithm for factoring numbers that, given input $n$,
tests each $k\in\set{2,\ldots\,n-1}$
to see if it divides~$n$.\footnote{%
  This is not the best algorithm, but a good illustration.}
If~$n$ is prime then it tests all those numbers, which is
a worst-case of roughly~$n$-many divisions.
The definition we are using
of input size is the number of bits that represent the input.
We know that the number of bits needed to represent~$n$ in binary
is approximately the base~$2$ logarithm, $\lg n$.
So for this algorithm the input is of size about~$\lg n$
and the number of operations is about~$n$.
That makes the number of operations exponential in the input
size\Dash this algorithm is
$\bigOh(2^b)$ where $b$ is the number of input bits.

However, most people's experience from programming class is that this
algorithm would be described as linear, as $\bigOh(n)$.
How to reconcile the two?

In most programming situations an input 
fits into a fixed sized computer word, such as a $64$~bit word.
Suppose that we are studying selection sort.
With $n$ integer elements of input, the number of bits is $64n$.
Analysis of selection sort gives that the time it takes grows quadratically
so in bits we get $\bigOh((64n)^2)$.
Ignoring constants gives that as the same as $\bigOh(n^2)$.

The same would hold in programming a depth-first tree traversal.
If your nodes are represented as successive integers and your
locations are 64 bits then you can store awfully big trees, probably
bigger than any tree you would ever encounter or have the time to process.
But that fixed-size input storage
decision changes the analysis of the relationship between
input size and output size, since now the input size is measured as
a function of the number of nodes~$n$.

So in this common programming situation, where the bit size of the
inputs is bounded,
the runtime behavior is most conveniently described as a function of
the number of inputs~$n$,
not of the bit size of those inputs.

An algorithm whose behavior as a function of the input is polynomial,
but whose behavior as a function of the bit size of the input is
exponential, is said to be \definend{pseudopolynomial}.
The factoring algorithm given earlier is an example.
% Such an algorithm may be acceptable for fixed-sized inputs, but
% is not acceptable for arbitrary inputs.

One final point about describing algorithm behavior with big-O.
When we are analyzing an algorithm we can look for a number of
different behavior descriptions.
For instance, we can describe the time the algorithm takes, or the space.
But we can also analyze the worst case behavior, or the average behavior.
For example, the widely used sorting algorithm quicksort
takes quadratic time $\bigOh(n^2)$ at worst.
But on average it is fast, $\bigOh(n\lg n)$.
So, algorithm analysis can happen on a number of fronts.

a problem is

if the best algorithm's resource consumption is subexponential,
so that it is conceivably practical.
A problem is \definend{intractable} if the best algorithm 
is superpolynomial.
In this part of the book we consider which problems are inheriently
intractable,
and which are open to analysis that makes them tractable.

\section{Time complexity}
We are interested in classifying various problems: for which problems
are there relatively good algorithms, and for which problems are there not?
Given a problem we will be interested in its best solution.

\begin{definition}
Let $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$.
A language~$\lang{L}$ is an element of \definend{$\DTIME(t)$}
if there is a deterministic
Turing machine that decides~$\lang{L}$ and runs in time~$\bigOh(t)$, and
that language is an element of \definend{$\DTIME(t)$}
if there is a nondeterministic
Turing machine that decides~$\lang{L}$ and runs in time~$\bigOh(t)$. 
\end{definition}

This does not say that an algorithm is in that class,
rather it says that a language is in that class.
A language is in that class if there is an algorithm for its decision problem
that has the stated big-O runtime behavior.

\begin{definition}
A language is in 
the complexity class \definend{polynomial time $\compclass{P}$},
often read \definend{polytime},
if it is a member of $\DTIME(n^c)$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{equation*}
  \compclass{P} =\bigcup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(n^c)          
     =\DTIME(n)\cup\DTIME(n^2)\cup\DTIME(n^3)\cup\,\cdots
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
A language is in 
the complexity class \definend{exponential time $\EXP$}
if it is a member of $\DTIME(2^{n^c})$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{equation*}
  \EXP =\bigcup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(2^{n^c})           
       =\DTIME(2^n)\cup\DTIME(2^{n^2})\cup\DTIME(2^{n^3})\cup\,\cdots
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

As discussed in the prior subsection,
polytime is our first take at can-conceivably-be-used,
and we say that the problem is `tractable'. 
in contrast, $\EXP$ as too slow; we can't compute it.
We say it is `intractable'.
This view of the matter began with J~Edmunds.

\begin{remark}
A natural concern is that if we change the computing model 
then we get different results.
What if a problem is $\DTIME(n^3)$ for a 
two-tape Turing machine but $\DTIME(n^5)$ for a one-tape 
machine?.

Experience with a wide range of alternative computational models is that
in almost all cases a Turing machine can be simulated on such a different model
machine in time~$\bigOh(n^2)$, and in any event can  be done
in polytime.
So if we have polytime sloppiness built in then we can essentially
ignore the model issue. 
\end{remark}









\section{Complexity classes}

 
\begin{definition}[the complexity class $\compclass{NP}$]
A language $\lang{L}$ is a member of 
$\compclass{NP}$ if there is a polynomial $\map{p}{\N}{\N}$ and a
polynomial time Turing machine $\TM$, the \definend{verifier}
for $\lang{L}$, such that 
\begin{equation*}
  \forall \sigma\in\strings 
  \big[ \sigma\in\lang{L} 
     \,\Longleftrightarrow\, 
     \exists w\in \set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}(\TM(\seq{\sigma,w})=1)  \big]
\end{equation*}
($w$ is the \definend{witness} or \definend{certificate} for $\sigma$).
\end{definition}

Note that the witness is restricted in length so that the verifier,
which as a Turing machine reads one bit at a time, can 
read it through in polynomial time.
Note also that we can take the polynomial~$p$ to be trivial $p(i)=0$
to get that $\compclass{P}\subseteq\compclass{NP}$.

\begin{example}
The satisfiability problem is in~$\compclass{NP}$.
That is, $\lang{L}\in\compclass{NP}$ where this is $\lang{L}$.
\begin{equation*}
  \lang{L}=\set{\varphi\suchthat 
             \text{$\varphi$ is a satisfiable propositional logic formula}}
\end{equation*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Formally, a mathematical proof as a sequence of statements,
$\sigma_0$, $\sigma_1$, \ldots, $\sigma_k$,
where the final statement is the conclusion. 
Each statement either is itself an axiom or
else follows from the statements before it
by an application
of one of the agreed-upon derivation rules.
From experience we know that finding the proof of a desired  
conclusion can be very hard; often it seems to take a leap of insight.
Trying to brute-force it by taking all combinations of 
all the axioms and all the derivation rules would be very slow.
In contrast, if we were given the 
sequence of statements along with a description
of each step's rules, and asked only to check the 
sequence for correctness,
then that would be easy and quick.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider a complicated propositional logic expression, say
$(\neg(P_1\vee (P_2\wedge P_3)\rightarrow \neg P_4)\leftrightarrow (P_2\rightarrow P_3)\rightarrow (P_1\wedge \neg P_4)$.
We want to find whether it is \definend{satisfiable}, 
whether there is any assignment of 
truth values $\true$ or~$\false$ to the 
variables $P_1,\idots, P_4$ that makes the statement
as a whole have the value $\true$.
We can imagine trying to answer by reasoning
in some way as, 
``Well, the formula has an if and only if arrow so I can see that
it boils down to \ldots''
but an algorithm that unfailingly finds the solution that way is not evident.
Alternatively, 
you could go through the possible assignments
$\seq{\false, \false, \false, \false}$, 
then $\seq{\false, \false, \false, \true}$, etc., 
until one causes the statement to yield $\true$, if one ever does.
However, if instead you were given an answer, an assignment to the variables,
and only asked to verify that it solves the problem, then you 
could do that quickly.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Recall that a graph is $k$-colorable if we can divide its vertices
into $k$-many classes, called colors because that's how they
are pictured, so that there is no edge between two same-colored
vertices.
\begin{animatedfigure}{A $3$-coloring of \protect\citetext{a graph}{This is the Petersen graph, a rich source of counterexamples for conjectures in Graph Theory}.}\centering

We will show that a quick way to solve the Satisfiability problem
would give us a quick way to solve Graph Colorability, that is,
we will show that $\text{Graph Colorability}\leq\text{Satisfiability}$.
To do this we need to translate facts about graphs into facts about
a propositional logic statement.

We illustrate with the problem of $3$-colorability.
Given a graph, suppose that it has $n$~vertices $\set{v_0,\ldots\, v_{n-1}}$.
We want to divide them into three classes, the first, second, and third colors.
Begin the translation by 
creating three types of boolean variables for the propositional logic clauses.
\begin{equation*}
  {f_0,\ldots f_{n-1}}
  \qquad
  {s_0,\ldots s_{n-1}}
  \qquad
  {t_0,\ldots t_{n-1}}
  \end{equation*}
If there is a $3$-coloring and 
vertex~$v_i$ has the first color then $f_i=T$, $s_i=F$, and $t_i=F$.
If instead vertex~$v_i$ has the second color then
$f_i=F$, $s_i=T$, and $t_i=F$.
And if vertex~$v_i$ has the third color then  $f_i=F$, $s_i=F$, and $t_i=T$.

Next, for each $i\in\set{0,\ldots\,n-1}$ create a clause ensuring that
vertex~$v_i$ gets some color.
\begin{equation*}
  (f_i\vee s_i \vee t_i)
\end{equation*}
Finally, for each of the graph's edges~$\set{v_i,v_j}$
create three clauses that together ensure that the edge does not connect
two same-color vertices.
\begin{equation*}
  (\neg f_i\wedge \neg f_j)
  \qquad
  (\neg s_i\wedge \neg s_j)
  \qquad
  (\neg t_i\wedge \neg t_j)
\end{equation*}
Now consider the conjunction of those clauses, the
propositional logic statement that joins them together with $\wedge$'s.
The graph has a $3$-coloring if and only if that statement
is satisfiable\Dash if and only if
there is a set of boolean values for the variables making all the clauses true.
\end{example}

\extra{The Factoring Problem and the Web}

One of the great things about the interwebs,
besides that you can get free Theory of Computing books,
is that can buy stuff.
You send a credit card number and a couple of days later
your stuff appears.

Obviously, to be practical this requires that your
credit card number must be kept secret.
It must be encrypted.

When you visit a web site using a \url{https}
address, that site sends you information, called a key, that your browser
uses to encrypt your card number.
The web site then uses a different key to decrypt.
This is an important point:~the decrypter must differ from
than the encrypter since
anyone on the net can see the encrypter information that the site sent you.
But the site keeps the decrypter information private.
These two, encrypter and decrypter, form a matched pair.
We will describe the mathematical technologies that make this work.



\subsection{The arithmetic}
We can view that everything on a modern computer is numbers.
Consider the message `send money'. 
Its ASCII encoding is \str{115} \str{101} \str{110} \str{100} \str{32}
\str{109} \str{111} \str{110} \str{101} \str{121}.

\str{01110011}\, \str{01100101}\, \str{01101110}\, \str{01100100}\,
\str{00100000}\, \str{01101101}\, \str{01101111}\, \str{01101110}\,
\str{01100101}\, \str{01111001}.
In decimal that's
% $544943221199950100456825$
$544\,943\,221\,199\,950\,100\,456\,825$.
So there is no loss in generality in
viewing everything we do, including encryption systems, as numerical operations.

To make such systems, mathematicians and computer scientists have
leveraged that there are
there are things we can do easily, but that we do not know how to easily
undo\Dash

We will describe an algorithm based on the Factoring Problem.
We have algorithms for multiplying numbers that are fast.
The algorithms that
we have for starting with a number and decomposing it into factors
are, by comparison, quite slow.
To illustrate this, you might contrast the time it takes you to multiply
two four-digit numbers by hand with the time it takes you to factor an
eight-digit number chosen at random.
Set aside an afternoon for that second job, it'll take a while.

Fermat's Little Theorem.
Adleman was unable to break it since, he said, 
it seemed that
only solving the Factoring problem would break it and no one knew how to
do that.

\citetext{tremendous amount of interest and excitement}{%

The basis of RSA is to find three numbers, a \definend{modulus}~$n$,
an \definend{encrypter}~$e$, and a \definend{decrypter}~$d$,
related by this equation
(here $m$ is the message, as a number).
\begin{equation*}
(m^{e})^{d}\equiv m \pmod {n} 
\end{equation*}
The encrypted message is $m^e\bmod{n}$.
To decrypt it, to recover~$m$, calculate $(m^e)^d\bmod{n}$.
These three are chosen so that
knowing $e$ and~$n$, or even~$m$, still leaves a potential secret-cracker 
who is looking for~$d$ with an extremely difficult job.

To choose them,
first choose distinct prime numbers $p$ and~$q$.
Pick these at random so they are of about equal bit-lengths.
Compute $n=pq$ and $\varphi(n)=(p-1)\cdot(q-1)$.
Next, choose~$e$ with $1<e<\varphi(n)$ that is realtively prime to~$n$.
Finally, find $d$ as the multiplicative inverse of~$e$ modulo~$n$.
(We shall show below that all these operations, including using the
keys for encryption and decryption, can be done quickly.)

The pair $\sequence{n,e}$ is the \definend{public key}
and the pair $\sequence{n,d}$ is the \definend{private key}.
The length of $d$ in bits is the \definend{key length}.
Most experts consider a key length of $2\,048$ bits to be
secure for the mid-term future, until 2030 or so,
when computers will have grown in power enough that they may be able
to use an exhaustive brute-force search to find~$d$.

\begin{example}
Alice chooses the primes $p=101$ and~$q=113$
(these are too small to use in practice but are good for an
illustration) and then calculates
$n=pq=11\,413$ and $\varphi(n)=(p-1)(q-1)=11\,200$.
To get the encrypter she randomly picks numbers $1<e<11\,200$ until she
gets one that is
relatively prime to $11\,200$, choosing $e=3533$.
She publishes her public key $\sequence{n,e}=\sequence{11\,413, 3\,533}$
on her home page.
She computes the decrypter $d=e^{-1}\bmod{11\,200}=6\,597$, and
finds a safe place to store her private key
$\sequence{n,d}=\sequence{11\,413,6\,597}$.

Bob wants to say `Hi'.
In ASCII that's \str{01001000} \str{01101001}.
If he converted that string into a single decimal number it would be bigger
than~$n$ so he breaks it into
two substrings, getting the decimals \str{72} and \str{105}.
Using her public key he computes
\begin{equation*}
  72^{3533}\bmod{11413}=10496
  \qquad
  105^{35333}\bmod{11413}=4861
\end{equation*}
and sends Alice the sequence $\sequence{10496,4861}$.
Alice recovers his message by using her private key.
\begin{equation*}
  10496^{6597}\bmod{11413} = 72
  \qquad
  4861^{6597}\bmod{11413} = 105
\end{equation*}
\end{example}

\subsection{The arithmetic, fast}
We've just illustrated that RSA uses invertible operations.
There are lots of ways to get invertible operations
so our understanding of RSA is incomplete unless we know why it uses these
particular operations.
As discussed above, the important point is that they can be done quickly,
but undoing them, finding the decrypter, is believed to take a very
long time.

We start with a classic, beautiful, result from Number Theory.

\begin{theorem}[Prime Number Theorem]
The number of primes less than $n\in\N$ is approximately
$n/\ln(n)$; that is, this limit is~$1$.
\begin{equation*} 
  \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\text{number of primes less than $x$}}{(x/\ln x)}
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
This shows the number of primes less than~$n$ for some values up to a million.
\begin{center}
  \includegraphics{complexity/asy/xygraphs/xygraphs00.pdf}
\end{center}
This theorem says that primes are common.
For example, the number of primes less than $2^{1024}$ is about
$2^{1024}/\ln(2^{1024})
  \approx 2^{1014}/709.78
  \approx 2^{1024}/2^{9.47}
  \approx 2^{1015}$.
% sage: 1024*ln(2)
% 1024*log(2)
% sage: 1024*ln(2).n()
% 709.782712893384
% sage: ln(1024*ln(2))/ln(2)
% log(1024*log(2))/log(2)
% sage: (ln(1024*ln(2))/ln(2)).n()
% 9.47123362705510
% sage: 1024-(ln(1024*ln(2))/ln(2)).n()
% 1014.52876637294
% sage: 1/(1024*ln(2))  
Said another way, if we choose a number~$n$ at random then the probability
that it is prime is about $1/\ln(n)$ and so 
a random number that is $1024$ bits long will be a prime with probability
about $1/(\ln(2^{1024}))\approx 1/710$.
% sage: 1/(1/(1024*ln(2)).n())
% 709.782712893384
On average
we need only select $355$ odd numbers of about that size 
before we find a prime.
Hence we can efficiently generate large primes by just picking random numbers,
as long as we can efficiently test their primality.

On our way to giving an efficient way to test primality,
we observe that the operations of multiplication and addition
modulo~$m$ are efficient.
(We will give examples only, rather than the full analysis
of the operations.)

\begin{example}
Multiplying $3\,915\,421$ by $52\,567\,004$
modulo~$3$ looks hard.
The naive approach is to first take their product and then 
divide by~$3$ to find the remainder.

But there is a more efficient way.
Rather than multiply first and then reduce modulo~$m$, reduce first
and then multiply.
That is, we know that if $a\equiv b\pmod{m}$ and 
$c\equiv d\pmod{m}$ then $ac\equiv bd\pmod{m}$ and so
Since $3\,915\,421\equiv 1\pmod{3}$
and $52\,567\,004\equiv 2\pmod{3}$ we have this.
\begin{equation*}
  3\,915\,421\cdot 52\,567\,004 
  \equiv 1\cdot 2\pmod{3} 
\end{equation*}
% In this way, multiplication modulo~$m$ is efficient in both time and space.
\end{example}

Similarly, exponentiation modulo $m$ is also efficient,
both in time and in space.

\begin{example}
Consider raising $4$ to the $13$-th power, modulo~$m=497$.
The naive approach would be to raise $4$ to the $13$-th power,
which is a very large number, and reduce
modulo~$497$.  
But there is a better way. 

Start by expressing the power~$13$ in base~$2$ as $13=8+4+1=1101_2$.
So, $4^{13}=4^{8}\cdot 4^4\cdot 4^1$ and we need the $8$-th power,
the $4$-th power, and the first power of~$4$.
If we can efficiently get those powers
then we can multiply them modulo~$m$ efficiently, so
we will be set.

Get the powers by repeated squaring (modulo~$m$).
Start with $p=1$.
Squaring gives $4^2$, then squaring again gives $4^4$, and
squaring again gives $4^8$.
Getting these powers (modulo~$m$) just requires a multiplication,
which we can do efficiently.
\end{example}

The last thing we need for efficiently testing primality
is to efficiently find the multiplicative inverse modulo~$m$.
Recall that two numbers are
\definend{relatively prime} or \definend{coprime}
if their greatest common divisor is~$1$.
For example, $15=3\cdot 5$ and $22=2\cdot 11$ are relatively prime.

\begin{lemma}
If $a$ and~$m$ are relatively prime then there is an inverse for~$a$ 
modulo~$m$, a number~$k$ such that $a\cdot k\equiv 1\pmod{m}$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Because the greatest common divisor of $a$ and~$m$ is~$1$, Euclid's algorithm 
gives a linear combination of the two, a $sa+tm$ for some $s,t\in\Z$, 
that adds to~$1$.
Doing the operations modulo~$m$ gives $sa+tm\equiv 1\pmod{m}$.
Since $tm$ is a multiple of~$m$, we have $tm\equiv 0\pmod m$, leaving 
$sa\equiv 1\pmod m$, and
$s$ is the inverse of~$a$ modulo~$m$.
\end{proof}

Euclid's algorithm is efficient, both in time and space,
so finding an inverse modulo~$m$ is efficient.

Now we can test for primes.
The simplest way to test whether a number~$n$ is prime is to try  
dividing~$n$ by all possible factors.
But that is very slow.
There is a faster way, based on the next result.

\begin{theorem}[Fermat] For a prime~$p$, if $a\in\Z$
is not divisible by~$p$ then $a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $a$ be an integer not divisible by the prime~$p$.
Multiply~$a$ by each number $i\in\set{1,\ldots\,p-1}$ and
reduce modulo~$p$ to get the numbers $r_i=ia\bmod{p}$.

We will show that the set $R=\set{r_1,\ldots\,r_{p-1}}$
equals the set $P=\set{1,\ldots\,p-1}$.
First, $R\subseteq P$.
Because $p$ is prime and does not divide $i$ or~$a$, it does not divide their
product~$ia$.
Thus $r_i=ia\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}$ and so all the $r_i$ are members of the
set $\set{1,\ldots\,p-1}$.

To get inclusion the other way, $P\subseteq R$, note that
if $i_0\neq i_1$ then $r_{i_0}\neq r_{i_1}$.
For, with $r_0-r_1=i_0a-i_1a=(i_0-i_1)a$, because $p$ is prime and
does not divide $i_0-i_1$ or~$a$ (as each is smaller in absolute value
than~$p$), it does not divide their product.
That means that the two sets have the same number of elements, so
$P\subseteq R$.

Now multiply together all of the elements of that set.
\begin{align*}
  a\cdot 2a\cdots (p-1) a
    &\equiv 1\cdot 2\cdots\,(p-1)\pmod{p} \\
  (p-1)!\cdot a^{p-1}
    &\equiv (p-1)!\pmod{p} \\
\end{align*}
Cancelling the $(p-1)!$'s gives the result.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
Let the prime be $p=7$.
Any number $a$ with $0<a<p$ is not divisible by~$p$.
Here is the list.

                  &\multicolumn{1}{c}{$5$}       
                  &\multicolumn{1}{c}{$6$} \\ \cline{1-7} 
    $a^{7-1}$\rule{0em}{2.5ex} 
             &$1$ &$64$
                  &$729$
                  &$4\,096$

    $(a^{6}-1)/7$
             &$0$ &$9$

  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
For instance, $15\,625=7\cdot 2\,232+1$.
\end{example}

Given~$n$, if we find a base~$a$ with~$0<a<n$ so that 
$a^{n-1}\bmod{n}$ is not~$1$ then $n$ is not prime.

\begin{example}
Consider $n=415\,692$.
If $a=2$ then
$2^{415692}\equiv 58346\pmod{415693}$
so $n$ is not prime.
\end{example}

There are $n$'s where $a^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$ but $n$ is not prime.
Such a number is a \definend{Fermat liar} or \definend{Fermat pseudoprime}
with base~$a$.
One for base $a=2$ is $n = 341=11\cdot 31$.
However, \citetext{computer searches suggest that these are very rare}{%
  For instance, among the numbers less than $2.5\times 10^{10}$ 
  there are only $21\,853\approx 2.2\times 10^{4}$ pseudoprimes base $2$;
  that's six orders of magnitude.}.

The rarity of exceptions suggests that we use a
\definend{probabilistic primality test}:~given $n\in\N$ to test for
primality,
pick at random a base~$a$ with $0<a<n$ and calculate whether
$a^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
If that is not true then $n$ is not prime.\footnote{%
  In this case we say that $a$ is a \protect\definend{witness} or
  \protect\definend{certificate} of the fact that the number~$n$
  is not prime.}
If it is true then we have evidence that~$n$ is prime.

Researchers have shown that if $n$ is not prime then
each choice of base~$a$ has a greater than $1/2$ chance of finding that
$a^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
So if $n$ were not prime and we did the test with two different bases
$a_0,a_1$ then
there would be a less than $(1/2)^2$ chance of getting both
$a_0^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$ and $a_1^n-1\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
So we figure that there is at least a $1-(1/2)^2$ chance that $n$ is prime. 
After $k$-many iterations of choosing a base, doing the
calculation, and never finding that that $n$ is not prime, then
we have a greater than $1-(1/2)^k$ chance that~$n$ is prime.

In summary,
if~$n$ passes $k$-many tests for \citetext{any reasonable-sized~$k$}{%
  Selecting an appropriate $k$ is an engineering choice between the cost
  of extra iterations and the gain in confidence.}
then \citetext{we are quite confident that it is prime}{%
  There are numbers, called \definend{Carmichael numbers})
  that are pseudoprime for every base~$a$ relatively prime to~$n$.
  The smallest example is $n = 561 = 3\cdot 11\cdot17$,
  and the next two are $1\,105$ and~$1\,729$.
  Like pseudoprimes, these seem to be very rare.
  Among the numbers less than $10^{16}$ there are about
  $2.7\times 10^{14}$ primes but only
  $246\ 683 \approx 2.4\times 10^5$-many Carmichael numbers.}.
Our interest in this
test is that it is extremely fast; it runs in time
$\bigOh (k \cdot (\log n)^2 \cdot \log \log n \cdot \log \log \log n)$.
So we can run it lots of times, becoming very confident, in not
very much time.

\begin{example}
We could test whether $n=7$ is prime by computing, say,  
that $3^{6}\equiv 1\pmod{7}$,
and $5^{6}\equiv 1\pmod{7}$, and $6^{6}\equiv 1\pmod{7}$.
The fact that $n=7$ does not fail makes us confident it is prime. 
\end{example}

The RSA algorithm also uses this offshoot of Fermat's Little Theorem.

\begin{corollary}
Let $p$ and~$q$ be unequal primes and
suppose that $a$ is not divisible by either one.
Then $a^{(p-1)(q-1)}\equiv 1\pmod{n}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Fermat, $a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p}$ and $a^{q-1}\equiv 1\pmod{q}$.
Raise the first to the $q-1$ power and the second to the $p-1$ power.
\begin{equation*}
  a^{(p-1)(q-1)}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
  \qquad
  a^{(p-1)(q-1)}\equiv 1\pmod{q}
\end{equation*}
Since  $a^{(p-1)(q-1)}-1$ is divisible by both $p$ and~$q$,
it is divisible by their product $pq=n$.
\end{proof}

Experts think that the most likely attack on RSA encryption is by
factoring the modulus~$n$.
Anyone who factors~$n$ can use the same method as the
RSA key setup process to turn the encrypter~$e$
into the decrypter~$d$.
That's why~$n$ is taken to be the product of two large primes; it makes
factoring as hard as possible.

% So a fast algorithm to solve the Factoring problem would put RSA,
% the most widely used method for encryption on the Internet, at risk.
% There are published algorithms that are subexponential,
% that for $b$-bit numbers and for any $\varepsilon>0$
% are faster than $\bigOh((1+\varepsilon)^b)$.
% No published algorithm can factor all integers in polynomial
% time, i.e.\ in time $\bigOh(b^n)$ for some constant power~$n$.
% Most experts suspect that no such algorithm is possible, but there
% are no proofs.

There is a factoring algorithm that takes only $\bigOh(b^3)$~time
(and $\bigOh((b)$~space), called
Shor's algorithm. 
But it runs only on quantum computers.
No one has to this point built such a machine and 
whether such machines will ever become practical is not clear.
For instance, in 2001 the first seven-qubit quantum computer
became the first to run Shor's algorithm.
It factored the number $15$.
There has been progress in the interim, but not enough to put RSA at risk.

\begin{frame}{$\DTIME$}
We are interested in classifying various problems: for which problems
are there relatively good algorithms, and for which problems are there not?
Given a problem we will be interested in its best solution.

\pause
\dfn
Let $\map{t}{\N}{\N}$.
A language is an element of \alert{$\DTIME(t)$} if there is a 
Turing machine that decides the language and runs in time~$\bigo(t)$. 

This does not say that an algorithm is in that class,
rather it says that a language is in that class.
A language is in that class if there is an algorithm for its decision problem
that has the stated big-oh time behavior.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Polynomial time}
\dfn
A language is in 
the complexity class \alert{polynomial time} \alert{$\P$}
(often read \textit{polytime})
if it is a member of $\DTIME(n^c)$
for some power~$c\in\N$.
\begin{align*}
  \P &=\cup_{c\in\N}\DTIME(n^c)           \\
     &=\DTIME(n)\cup\DTIME(n^2)\cup\DTIME(n^3)\cup\,\cdots
\end{align*}

We take polytime as a first take at can-conceivably-be-run.
People say the problem is \alert{tractable}.

\rem
A natural concern is that if we change the computing model 
then we get different results.
We can imagine a situation where a problem is $\DTIME(n^3)$ for a 
two-tape Turing machine, and a $\DTIME(n^5)$ for a one-tape 
machine, or for Post's computation model.

Experience with a wide range of alternative computational models is that
in almost all cases a Turing machine can be simulated on such a different model
machine in time~$\bigo(n^2)$, and in any event can  be done
in polytime.
So if we have polytime sloppiness built in then we can essentially
ignore the model issue. 
\end{frame}

  \pause
  The naive approach is this.
\begin{lstlisting}
# relatively_prime.py
def gcd(a, b):
    r = 1
    m = min(a, b)
    for i in range(2, m):
        if  ((a % i == 0)
             and (b % i == 0)):
            r = i
    return r

a = int(raw_input("a? "))

\end{lstlisting}

the algorithm loops through \lstinline!range(2,16)!, 
approximately $16=2^4$-many values of~$i$.
So it runs in time that is $\bigo(2^n)$ where
$n$ is the input size.
\end{frame}
  
\begin{frame}[fragile]
In contrast, 
Euclid's algorithm is not exponential in the size of the input.
\begin{lstlisting}
def euclid_gcd(a, b):
    while (b != 0):
        t = b
        b = a % b
        a = t
    return a
\end{lstlisting}
It runs in time linear in the number of bits of the smaller number,
$\bigo(\lg(\min(a,b)))$.
Therefore $\lang\in\P$.

\begin{frame}{Alternate characterization of $\NP$}
\lem
A language~$\lang$ is a member of~$\NP$ if and only if there is
a polytime deterministic Turing machine~$\mach$, 
and a polynomial~$p$, such that for all strings $\sigma\in\ks{\set{0,1}}$
the following holds.
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma\in\lang
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \exists w(\sigma)\in\set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}\lbrack\mach(\sigma,w(\sigma))=1\rbrack
  \tag{$*$}
\end{equation*}

\smallskip
The string~$w(\sigma)$ is a \alert{witness} or \alert{certificate} for~$\sigma$.
Note that a single $\mach$ works for all~$\sigma$.

\pf
Suppose first that $\lang\in\NP$.
Then it is decided by some nondeterministic machine~$\mach$ whose running time
is a polynomial~$p$.
For any input $\sigma\in\lang$ there is a sequence of nondeterministic forks
in the computation history leading to acceptance by~$\mach$,
and this sequence~$w$ can serve as the witness
for~$\sigma$.
Note that $w$ has length $p(|\sigma|)$ and can be verified in polynomial
time by a deterministic machine simulating $\mach$, and
thus $\lang\in\NP$.\continued
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
For the other direction 
suppose that ($*$) holds; we will describe a polytime NDTM that 
decides~$\lang$.
The idea is that this NDTM tests every possible witness
$w\in\set{0,1}^{p(|\sigma|)}$, using the polytime 
$\mach(\sigma,w)$ to do so.
Because of the unbounded parallelism, the total time taken
is polytime. 
Because of the case assumption that ($*$) holds,
$\sigma\in\lang$ if and only if
the NDTM will find such a witness $w(\sigma)$. 
\qed
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Some $\protect\NP$-hard problems}
We won't give proofs of these; see Garey and~Johnson's book.

\begin{problemstatement}{Factoring}
Given a number $n\in\N$ and lower and upper bounds $b_L,b_U\in\N$.
Decide if the number has a prime factor in the interval.
\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the prime.
(We don't know if this problem is $\NP$-complete.)

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Travelling salesman}
Given a set of cities~$C=\set{c_0,\ldots,c_{n-1}}$,
a function $\map{d}{C\times C}{\N}$ 
giving the distance between any two cities,
and a natural number~$k$.
Decide if there is a tour of all the cities 
$\sequence{c_{i_0},\ldots,c_{i_{n-1}}}$
whose total distance is less than~$k$.
\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the circuit.
(This problem is known to be $\NP$-complete.)

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Crossword puzzle}
Given a set of $2n$-many words.
Decide if they can be arranged into an $n\times n$ array 
crossword.
\end{problemstatement}

The witness is the array.
(This problem is known to be $\NP$-complete.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The canonical six $\protect\NP$ complete problems}
We have already seen this $\NP$-complete problem.

\begin{problemstatement}{Satisfiability}
Given a set of propositional logic statements $\varphi_j$. 
Decide if there is 
an assignment of booleans $\set{\textsc{T},\textsc{F}}$ to the
variables making all the $\varphi_j$ true.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
This variation
is sometimes convenient and is also $\NP$-complete.

\begin{problemstatement}{Three-satisfiability ($3$-SAT)}
Given a set of propositional logic statements $\varphi_j$, 
each with no more than three variables. 
Decide if there is  
an assignment of booleans $\set{\textsc{T},\textsc{F}}$ to the
variables making all the $\varphi_j$ true.   
\end{problemstatement}

\rem Two-satisfiability is not $\NP$-complete.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
\begin{problemstatement}{Vertex cover}
Given a graph and a natural number bound~$b$. 
Decide if
there is a size~$b$ \alert{vertex cover}, a
subset of the vertices~$C$ such that for every edge
$\sequence{v,\hat{v}}$ an endpoint 
is in~$C$.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Clique}
Given a graph and a natural number bound~$b$.
Decide if the graph has a $b$-vertex \alert{clique},
a set of vertices such that every two are connected by an edge.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Hamiltonian circuit}
Given a graph. 
Decide if it has a \alert{Hamiltonian circuit},
an ordering of all the vertices
$\sequence{v_0,v_1\ldots,v_{n-1}}$ such that 
every pair $v_i$ and~$v_{i+1}$
are connected by an edge, and also connected are $v_{n-1}$ and~$v_0$.
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Three dimensional matching ($3$-DM)}
Given three disjoint finite sets 
$X$, $Y$, and~$Z$, and some $T\subseteq X\times Y\times Z$
(so $T$ consists of triples $\sequence{x,y,z}\in T$).
Decide if $T$ has a \alert{matching subset} $M\subseteq T$:~if 
$\sequence{x_0,y_0,z_0},\sequence{x_1,y_1,z_1} \in M$ then
$x_0\neq x_1$ and $y_0\neq y_1$ and~$z_0\neq z_1$. 
\end{problemstatement}

\pause
\begin{problemstatement}{Partition}
Given a multiset~$S\subset\N$ 
(a collection of natural numbers in which there may be repeats).
Decide if~$S$ can be \alert{partitioned} into 
two disjoint sub-multisets $S_0$ and~$S_1$ such that
the sum of the members of~$S_0$ equals the sum of the members of~$S_1$.
\end{problemstatement}
\end{frame}

At this time, we do not know 
whether  $\P=\NP$.
(As to whether $\NP=\EXP$, 
all currently-known algorithms for solving $\NP$-complete problems run
in (worst case) exponential time, but we do not konw if $\NP=\EXP$ either.)  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$P=NP$ guesses?}
The $\P=\NP$ question is one of the 
\href{http://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems}{Millennium Problems},
for which there is a $\$1,000,000$~reward.

\pause
Most experts believe that $\P\neq\NP$.
Arora and Barak put it this way.
\begin{quotation}\small
\noindent  
If you have ever eattempted a crossword puzzle, you konw that it is much 
harder to solve it from scratch than to verify a solution provided by 
someone else.
Likewise, solving a math homework problem  by yourself is usually much 
harder than reading and understanding a solution provided by your instructor.
The usual explanation for this difference of effort is that finding a solution 
\ldots\ 
requires \emph{creative effort}.
Verifying a solution is much easier since somebody else has already done
the cretaive part.
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
Scott Aaronson in 
\href{http://www.scottaaronson.com/blog/?p=122}{a 2006 blog post}
(and also a
\href{http://www.scottaaronson.com/blog/?p=1720}{2014 blog post})
gives many reasons: here are a few.
\begin{desc}
\item[The Obvious Argument.] After half a century, we still don't 
know any algorithm for an $\NP$-complete problem that runs in 
subexponential time.
\ldots
In math, if decades of research fail to turn up an object, 
and there's no a priori reason to suppose the object exists, 
it's usually a good strategy to conjecture that the object doesn't exist. 
\item[The Empirical Argument.] \ldots\ people certainly want fast ways 
to solve $\NP$-complete problems in practice, 
and they haven't been able to invent them.
\item[The Philosophical Argument.] If $\P=\NP$, then the world would be a 
profoundly different place than we usually assume it to be. 
There would be no special value in ``creative leaps,'' 
no fundamental gap between solving a problem and recognizing the solution 
once it's found. 
Everyone who could appreciate a symphony would be Mozart; 
everyone who could follow a step-by-step argument would be Gauss; 
everyone who could recognize a good investment strategy would be 
Warren Buffett.
\end{desc}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
Knuth has the opposite guess.

\begin{quotation} \small
I've come to believe that $\P = \NP$, \ldots\, 
Some of my reasoning is admittedly na\"ive: It's hard to believe that 
$\P\neq\NP$ and that so many brilliant people have failed to discover why.
\ldots

My main point, however, is that I don't believe that the equality 
$\P = \NP$ will turn out to be helpful even if it is proved, 
because such a proof will almost surely be nonconstructive. 
\ldots\ 
Mathematics is full of examples where something is proved to exist, 
yet the proof tells us nothing about how to find it. 
Knowledge of the mere existence of an algorithm is completely different 
from the knowledge of an actual algorithm.

More to the point, Robertson and Seymour have proved a famous theorem in graph 
theory: Any class of graphs that is closed under taking minors has a 
finite number of minor-minimal graphs. 
% (A minor of a graph is any graph obtainable by deleting vertices, deleting edges, or shrinking edges to a point. A minor-minimal graph H for is a graph whose smaller minors all belong to although H itself doesn't.) 
Therefore there exists a polynomial-time algorithm to decide whether or not 
a given graph belongs to that class: The algorithm checks that G doesn't 
contain any of the class's minor-minimal graphs as a minor.
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
\begin{quotation}\small
But we don't know what that algorithm is, except for a few special classes, 
because the set of minor-minimal graphs is often unknown. 
The algorithm exists, but it's not known to be discoverable in finite time.

% This consequence of Robertson and Seymour's theorem definitely surprised me, when I learned about it while reading a paper by LovÃ¡sz. And it tipped the balance, in my mind, toward the hypothesis that P = N P.

The moral is that people should distinguish between known (or knowable) 
polynomial-time algorithms and arbitrary polynomial-time algorithms. 
People might never be able to implement a polynomial-time-worst-case algorithm 
for satisfiability, even though $\P$ happens to equal $\NP$.
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}
