\chapter*{Preface}
The Theory of Computation is awfully nice.
It is beautiful.
It has deep connections to other areas of computer science and
mathematics, as well as to the wider intellectual world.
And, clearly one of the themes in the coming century 
will be the power of computation.
So it is timely, too. 

It makes a delightful course.
Students learn about the contrast between computation's capabilities 
and limits.
There are well-understood principles 
and within easy reach there are as-yet-unknown areas.

This text aims to mirror all of that\Dash to be rigorous, topical, insightful,
and perhaps even sometimes delightful.


\header{For students}
This field has a nearly hundred year history.
It is a rich story.
We will not see its complete detail of course but we will see it in
sufficient depth that you will have some perspective.

We do not stint on mathematics; everything here is proved. 
But you will find the story told in a liberal way, in a way that 
\citetext{aims for a breadth of knowledge, in addition to technical detail}{%
  \protect\url{https://www.aacu.org/leap/what-is-a-liberal-education}}.

The presentation here encourages you be an active learner, to
learn outside of the text and to
reflect on the motivation, development, and future of the ideas.
After reading the statement of each result, think of examples,
and also how you would approach the proof.
When you find a point particularly interesting, follow it.
The back of the book has lots of notes to the main text, which in turn
contain lots of links; check them out.

And, the Extra sections at the end of each chapter 
also help you to explore.
Whether or not your instructor covers them in the class schedule
(which can be tight), these topics will enrich
your understanding.

The subject is big, and a challenge.
But, it is a lot of fun.
Enjoy!


\header{For instructors}
This text covers the topics that are the current standard:
computability including Turing machines and unsolvability, 
languages and automata, and complexity.
The prerequisites are the topics covered in the standard Discrete Mathematics
course:~propositional logic and truth tables;
introduction to predicates;
proofs, including induction;
sets, functions, and relations.

Our approach differs from the standard one in a number of ways but
most noticeable is that we start with Turing machines.
After all, they are the touchstone.
In addition, we have pruned and shaped the material a bit
so that you have time to give Complexity a fair shake.

The coverage of Turing machines sets the tone.
We give a rigorous definition but we also 
discuss motivation and meaning.
For example, we include a discussion and justification of Church's Thesis. 

The remainder of the first part of the book
shows that there are problems that are unsolvable, both nonconstructively
with a counting argument and constructively with the Halting Problem.
We do Rice's Theorem, and include a discussion of 
how these results dovetail with the wider intellectual culture.

The next part does automata.
After background material on languages and graphs,
we do finite state machines.
This is our first encounter with nondeterminism so there is a discussion.
We cover Kleene's Theorem and the Pumping Lemma.
We finish by briefly covering pushdown machines
and context free languages.

Then we are on to complexity.
We cover polynomial and exponential time.
We raise the question of P versus~NP, with a number of benchmark examples.
We close by reflecting on the significance of these topics, 
including discussion of the practical aspects of the difference.

\header{Speaking in code}
Basing development on the prior work that students have done,
tying new ideas to a learner's existing knowledge, are 
sound teaching principles.
And surely expressing our thoughts clearly and succinctly is also sound.

We use Scheme as a single, uniform, practical language in which we 
\citetext{communicate our 
executable thoughts}{John McCarthy, 
the inventor of Scheme's ancestor Lisp, suggested the language
as well-suited for the Theory of Computation.
Two arguments in that direction 
are the language's elegance and expresiveness.
This is captured  an online obituary of
McCarthy written by Bertrand Meyer for the 
\textit{Communications of the Association for Computing Machinery}:
``The Lisp 1.5 manual, published in 1962, was another masterpiece; 
as early as page 13 it introduces\Dash an unbelievable feat, especially 
considering that the program takes hardly more than half a page\Dash an 
interpreter for the language being defined, written in that very language! 
The more recent reader can only experience here the kind of visceral, 
poignant and inextinguishable jealously that overwhelms us the first time we 
realize that we will never be able to attend the premi\`ere of Don Giovanni at 
the Estates Theater in Prague on 29 October, 1787 \ldots\,. 
What may have been the reaction of someone in `Data Processing,' 
such as it was in 1962, suddenly coming across such a language manual?'' 
\autocite{MeyerB}}.
As the quote below by Allen Downey expresses, modern programming languages are
a mix of precision and conscision that is hard to beat.
And, as Dijkstra's aphorism notes, Scheme is a delight.


\header{Exploration, Enrichment, Connections}
This book encourages readers to actively explore what we cover.
For one thing, 
each chapter ends with a number of topics that, while they are not in
the main line of our study, are fun and fascinating.
These are suitable as single day lectures, or to assign for group work
or for extra credit.

Another way to encourage exploration is to make stuff move.
The electronic version of the book
includes a number of animations; just click on them.

Still another way to encourage readers to keep going is to include links.
We have all gotten lost on the interwebs, moving from
document to document. 
Where practical, the references here are clickable.
For example, the pictures of the people who discovered the subject
are a link to their page on Wikipedia.
I hope readers enjoy these and I know that because it is linked-to,
the content is much more likely to be
seen than is the same content in the library.
