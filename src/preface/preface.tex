\chapter*{Preface}
The Theory of Computation is awfully nice.
It is beautiful.
It has deep connections to other areas of computer science and
mathematics, as well as to the wider intellectual world.
Oh, and clearly one of the themes in the coming century 
will be the power of computation
so it is timely, too. 

It makes a delightful course.
Students learn about the contrast between computation's capabilities 
and limits.
There are well-understood principles 
and within easy reach there are as-yet-unknown areas.

This text aims to mirror all of that\Dash to be rigorous, topical, insightful,
and perhaps even a bit delightful.


\header{For students}
This subject has nearly a hundred year history, and it
makes a rich story.
We will not see it in its complete detail of course, but in
sufficient depth that you can understand both its main threads and how
it applies to other areas.

Although we do not stint on technical details where they are called for, 
you will find the story told here in a liberal way, in a way that 
\citetext{along with an in-depth explication of key technical results, also
  strives for a breadth of knowledge}{%
  \protect\url{https://www.aacu.org/leap/what-is-a-liberal-education}}.



\header{For instructors}
We cover the standard topics\Dash
Turing machines, unsolvability, 
automata, languages, and complexity\Dash although 
we have pruned and shaped a bit,
to ensure that there is time to give Complexity its fair shake.

Our approach differs from the standard one in a number of ways.
First, we start with Turing machines.
They are the touchstone.
We give a rigorous definition but we also 
include extensive discussion, including a discussion of Church's Thesis. 

Next 
we show that there are problems that are unsolvable, both nonconstructively
with a counting argument and constructively with the Halting Problem.
We do Rice's Theorem, and include a discussion of 
the effect of these results on wider intellectual culture.

Then, after covering languages and graphs as a prerequisite,
we do automata, mostly finite state machines.
This is our first encounter with nondeterminism so we include a discussion.
We do Kleene's Theorem and the Pumping Lemma.
We finish this part by briefly covering pushdown machines
and context free languages.
We also discuss the practical use of regular expressions.

Finally, it is on to complexity.
We cover polynomial time problems, as well as others.
We raise P versus~NP, with a number of examples.
We close by discussing the significance of these topics, 
including discussion of the practical aspects of the difference.

\header{Expressing algorithms in code}
Basing development on the prior work that students have done,
tying new ideas to a learner's existing knowledge, are 
sound teaching principles.
And surely expressing our thoughts clearly and succinctly is also a 
sound teaching principle.

We use Scheme as a single, uniform, practical language in which we 
\citetext{express our 
algorithmic thoughts}{John McCarthy, 
the inventor of Scheme's ancestor Lisp, suggested the language
as well-suited for the Theory of Computation.
Two arguments in that direction 
are the language's elegance and expresiveness.
This is captured  an online obituary of
McCarthy written by Bertrand Meyer for the 
\textit{Communications of the Association for Computing Machinery}:
``The Lisp 1.5 manual, published in 1962, was another masterpiece; 
as early as page 13 it introduces\Dash an unbelievable feat, especially 
considering that the program takes hardly more than half a page\Dash an 
interpreter for the language being defined, written in that very language! 
The more recent reader can only experience here the kind of visceral, 
poignant and inextinguishable jealously that overwhelms us the first time we 
realize that we will never be able to attend the premi\`ere of Don Giovanni at 
the Estates Theater in Prague on 29 October, 1787 \ldots\,. 
What may have been the reaction of someone in `Data Processing,' 
such as it was in 1962, suddenly coming across such a language manual?'' 
\autocite{MeyerB}}.
As the quote below by Allen Downey expresses, modern programming languages are
a mix of precision and conscision that is hard to beat.
And, as Dijkstra's aphorism notes, Scheme is a delight.


\header{Links}
This book encourages readers to explore the topics that we cover.

For one thing, 
each chapter ends with a number of topics that, while they are not in
the main line of our study, are fun and fascinating.
These are suitable as single day lectures, or to assign for group work
or for extra credit.

Another way to encourage exploration is to make stuff move.
The book includes a number of animations; just click on them.

Still another way is to include links.
There are two kinds.
Many things in the book's body are cited in the back for more
discussion.
I have tried to make these small discussions be enlargements that
students will find intruiging.
The other kind of links are web links.
Where sensible, the references are clickable.
For example, the pictures of the people who discovered the subject
are a link to their Wikipedia page.
I hope readers enjoy them, and I know that they are more likely to be
seen than is the same content in a book in the library.
