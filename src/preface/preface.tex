\par\noindent{\Large\bfseries Preface}
\vspace{3ex}

The Theory of Computation is awfully nice.
It is beautiful.
It has deep connections to other areas of computer science and
mathematics, as well as to the wider intellectual world.
And clearly one of the themes in the coming century 
will be the power of computation.
So it is timely, too. 

It also makes a delightful course.
Its organizing question\Dash what can be done?\Dash is both obviously natural
and compelling.
Students learn about the contrast between computation's capabilities 
and limits.
There are well-understood principles 
and within easy reach are as-yet-unknown areas.

This text aims to reflect all of that\Dash to be rigorous, topical, insightful,
and perhaps sometimes even delightful.

\header{For students}
We will study computation, not computers.
We will be less interested in semicolons and cache misses than we are in
larger issues, such as
what can be computed with a mechanism,
both in principle and in practice.
This has been intensively studied for nearly a hundred years.
We will not see all of the details of course but we will see 
enough that you will end with an overview, with some
powerful insights.

We will be eager to make connections with other fields, and with
other modes of thinking but always we will approach things from the
computational perspective, that there are organizing principles in
computation and seeing how those principles hold across the details
of different models gives rise to a deep understanding.

We do not stint on mathematics; we take proofs as our good standard. 
But you will find the story told in a liberal way, in a way that 
\citetext{aims for a breadth of knowledge in addition to technical detail}{%
  \protect\url{https://www.aacu.org/leap/what-is-a-liberal-education}}.

The presentation here encourages you be an active learner, to
work with the ideas outside of the text and to
reflect on the motivation, development, and future of those ideas.
One way it encourages those things is by emphasizing motivation and naturalness.
Another is by encouraging you to follow up on things you find particularly
interesting.
For instance, the back of the book has lots of notes to the main text,
which in turn contain lots of links to check out.
And, the Extra sections at the end of each chapter 
also help you to explore.
Whether or not your instructor covers them in the class schedule
(which can be tight), these topics will enrich
your understanding.

The subject is big, and a challenge.
But it is also lots of fun.
Enjoy!

\header{For instructors}
This text covers the topic that have become standard:~computability
including Turing machines and unsolvability, 
languages and automata, and complexity.
The audience is computer science majors, as well as majors in
nearby areas such as mathematics.

The prerequisites are covered in the standard Discrete Mathematics
course:~propositional logic and truth tables,
predicates,
proof methods including induction,
graphs,
basic number theory such as primes, factoring, and modular arithmetic, and
sets, functions, and relations.

% Some of the students of the theory of computation are aiming, when they
% are finished, at more advanced study.
% There are a number texts that would be an excellent choice for those folks.
% But there are also students who aim in other directions and this
% text has them in mind as a primary audience.
% These are computer science and mathematics majors, bright and capable people,
% who have considerable experience
% with practical computing, particularly programming, as well as in
% a number of nearby areas.
% But they do not have the same need to move through a volume of material;
% for these folks it is better to make the main points well
% than to make many points.


Our presentation differs from the standard one in a number of ways but
most noticeable is the liberal approach: we aim to give a real understanding
of the results both by stressing motivation and naturalness
and, where practical, by setting the results in a context.

One example of this difference is that we start with Turing machines.
After all, they are the field's touchstone.
The coverage of this topic sets the tone.
We give a rigorous definition but we also 
discuss motivation and meaning.
For example, we include a discussion and justification of Church's Thesis. 

The remainder of the first part of the book
shows that there are problems that are unsolvable, both nonconstructively
with a counting argument and constructively with the Halting Problem.
We do Rice's Theorem, and include a discussion of 
how these results with the wider intellectual culture of their times.

Next comes automata.
After background material on languages and graphs
(which may be review),
we do finite state machines.
This is the first encounter with nondeterminism so we discuss it.
We see Kleene's Theorem and the Pumping Lemma, and
finish by briefly covering pushdown machines
and context free languages.

Then, complexity.
After background material on big-O,
we define $\P$ and $\NP$ and raise
the question of their equality.
We close by reflecting on the significance of these topics.

\header{Speaking in code}
% Basing development on the prior work that students have done,
% tying new ideas to a learner's existing knowledge, are 
% sound teaching principles.
% And surely expressing our thoughts clearly and succinctly is also sound.
%
As the quote below by Allen Downey expresses, for communicating
effective procedures,
modern programming languages are
a mix of precision and conscision that is hard to beat.
We use Scheme as a single, uniform, practical language for this. 
One reason is that it is very easy to learn.
Another is that it
\citetext{suits the topics}{John McCarthy, 
the inventor of Scheme's ancestor Lisp, suggested the language
as well-suited for the Theory of Computation.
Two arguments in that direction 
are the language's elegance and expresiveness.
This is captured  an online obituary of
McCarthy written by Bertrand Meyer for the 
\textit{Communications of the Association for Computing Machinery}:
``The Lisp 1.5 manual, published in 1962, was another masterpiece; 
as early as page 13 it introduces\Dash an unbelievable feat, especially 
considering that the program takes hardly more than half a page\Dash an 
interpreter for the language being defined, written in that very language! 
The more recent reader can only experience here the kind of visceral, 
poignant and inextinguishable jealously that overwhelms us the first time we 
realize that we will never be able to attend the premi\`ere of Don Giovanni at 
the Estates Theater in Prague on 29 October, 1787 \ldots\,. 
What may have been the reaction of someone in `Data Processing,' 
such as it was in 1962, suddenly coming across such a language manual?'' 
\autocite{MeyerB}}.
But the main reason is that, as Dijkstra's aphorism notes, Scheme is a delight.

\header{Exploration, Enrichment, Connections}
This book encourages readers to actively explore what we cover.
For one thing, 
each chapter ends with a number of topics that, while they are not in
the main line of our study, are fun and fascinating.
These are suitable as single day lectures, or to assign for group work
or for extra credit.

Another way to encourage exploration is to make stuff move.
The electronic version of the book
includes a number of animations; just click on them.

Still another way to encourage readers to keep going is to include links.
We have all gotten lost on the interwebs, moving from
document to document. 
Where practical, the references here are clickable.
For example, the pictures of the people who discovered the subject
are a link to their page on Wikipedia.
I hope readers enjoy these and I know that because it is linked-to,
the content is much more likely to be
seen than is the same content in the library.
